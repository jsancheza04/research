## Q1. Does school-leaving age, graduation age or grade structure affect early labor outcomes and idleness?

### Takeaway
Three kinds of evidence point the same way. Graduating into a larger or more crowded cohort lowers early earnings (Ontario double cohort). Starting school older shifts the whole school-to-work timeline (Norway, Brazil). Raising compulsory schooling or participation ages cuts youth crime, though it does less for NEET status than expected (England's RPA).

I found no causal study that measures what happens to graduates who finish school *before* they reach the legal labor-market age, which is the "age-18 waiting room". This is a real gap in the literature.

### Cited Findings
**Ontario's abolition of grade 13 (the 2003 "double cohort")**
- Morin, L.-P. (2015), "Cohort size and youth earnings: Evidence from a quasi-experiment," *Labour Economics* 32: 99–111.
  - Design: Ontario removed grade 13, so two cohorts graduated from high school in 2003. This is a quasi-experimental shock to cohort size, analysed with LFS and Census data.
  - Results: Census data show weekly earnings about 5–9% lower. LFS data show a larger drop right after graduation.
  - Sources: [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:labeco:v:32:y:2015:i:c:p:99-111); [CRDCN summary](https://crdcn.ca/publication/cohort-size-and-youth-earnings-evidence-from-a-quasi-experiment/)
- The 2011 working-paper version reports a 14–25% wage drop in the six-month LFS results. — [IZA conference paper (Morin)](https://conference.iza.org/Transatlantic2011/morin_l3419.pdf)
- An undergraduate essay from Queen's (Kipouros) finds only weak evidence of persistent effects on unemployment and earnings. It is low weight. — [Queen's student paper](https://www.econ.queensu.ca/sites/econ.queensu.ca/files/student_papers/506.pdf)
- Not verified: other papers on the double cohort, including its effect on university admission competition and on the age at which students finish.

**School starting age (Norway)**
- Black, S., Devereux, P. & Salvanes, K. (2011), "Too Young to Leave the Nest? The Effects of School Starting Age," *Review of Economics and Statistics* 93(2): 455–467 (NBER WP 13969).
  - Design: Norwegian population registers, using birth-date cutoffs for school entry.
  - Results: Starting school older has a small negative effect on IQ at age 18. Earnings are lower until about age 30, which the authors read as less labour-market experience at any given age.
  - Sources: [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:tpr:restat:v:93:y:2011:i:2:p:455-467); [IDEAS NBER WP](https://ideas.repec.org/p/nbr/nberwo/13969.html)
- The paper also studies teenage pregnancy and educational attainment. I did not retrieve those results.

**School entry age (Brazil, RDD)**
- Children born just after the school-entry cutoff accumulate 0.36 more years of schooling ten years after enrolling. They finish high school about 8 months earlier but leave formal education about 0.3 years older.
- Early entry has no effect on formal employment but raises formal wages.
- Authors are listed as "Herdeiro and coauthors" in search output. I did not confirm the exact citation or outlet.
- Sources: [Insper repository](https://repositorio.insper.edu.br/entities/publication/5c24689f-1534-4f05-b2dc-6258f7f521de/full); possibly the same work at [Economic Development and Cultural Change, doi 10.1086/730190](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/730190), which is unconfirmed.

**Compulsory schooling and crime**
- Anderson, D. M. (2014), "In School and Out of Trouble? The Minimum Dropout Age and Juvenile Crime," *Review of Economics and Statistics* 96(2): 318–331.
  - Design: US county arrest data for 1980–2008, analysed with a triple-difference design across state minimum dropout ages.
  - Result: Higher minimum dropout ages significantly reduce property and violent crime arrests among 16–18-year-olds. This is an "incapacitation" or keep-them-busy effect.
  - Sources: [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/tpr/restat/v96y2014i2p318-331.html); [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:tpr:restat:v:96y:2014:i:2:p:318-331)
  - A related paper finds that some crime is displaced into schools ("The minimum dropout age and student victimization," *Economics of Education Review* 35, 2013). — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/ecoedu/v35y2013icp66-74.html)
- Machin, S., Marie, O. & Vujić, S. (2011), "The Crime Reducing Effect of Education," *Economic Journal* 121: 463–484.
  - Design: Changes to the school-leaving age in England and Wales, in a regression-discontinuity framework.
  - Result: Property crime falls significantly.
  - Sources: [LSE Research Online](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/28727); [IZA DP 5000](https://econpapers.repec.org/paper/izaizadps/dp5000.htm)

**England's Raising of the Participation Age (RPA) to 18 (2013/2015)**
- RPA requires education or training until 18. A Bath/IPR evaluation funded by the Nuffield Foundation finds only modest gains in education and training. — [Univ. of Bath announcement](https://www.bath.ac.uk/announcements/policy-to-keep-teens-in-education-until-18-has-limited-impact-study-finds/); [IPR policy brief](https://www.bath.ac.uk/publications/keeping-young-people-in-learning-until-the-age-of-18-does-it-work-policy-brief/attachments/ipr-policy-brief-keeping-young-people-in-learning-until-the-age-of-18.pdf)
- The sources disagree on NEET effects:
  - A Durham seminar abstract reports fewer *persistently* NEET young people. — [Durham seminar](https://www.durham.ac.uk/departments/academic/education/about-us/events/research-seminar-21may25)
  - FFT Education Datalab finds the cohort NEET share unchanged. It warns that the post-Wolf Review cut in vocational courses happened at the same time, which confounds the comparison. — [FFT Education Datalab](https://ffteducationdatalab.org.uk/2025/07/did-raising-the-participation-age-lead-to-increased-post-16-participation/)
  - FE Week coverage reports lower sustained participation and higher NEET. — [FE Week](https://feweek.co.uk/?p=146933)
  - I could not resolve this conflict from snippets alone.
- The earlier 1972 English school-leaving-age reform (ROSLA, from 15 to 16) improved qualifications, employment and earnings for affected cohorts, as cited in the IPR brief. — [IPR policy brief](https://www.bath.ac.uk/publications/keeping-young-people-in-learning-until-the-age-of-18-does-it-work-policy-brief/attachments/ipr-policy-brief-keeping-young-people-in-learning-until-the-age-of-18.pdf)

**Turkey: the 2012 compulsory-education law (12 years) and NEET**
- A 2025 ERF working paper uses a fuzzy RDD on NEET outcomes for ages 15–29. It notes that Erten & Keskin (2019) covered only ages 15–20. — [ERF WP (2025)](https://erf.org.eg/app/uploads/2025/08/1755606382_984_1091651_1788.pdf)
- I did not get the effect sizes or the authors' names from the snippets.

**Not verified**
- Oreopoulos (2006, AER) on UK compulsory schooling returns.
- Fredriksson & Öckert (2014, Economic Journal) on Swedish school-starting age and the timing of labor-market entry.
- Mexico's 2012 reform making upper secondary compulsory.

### Inferences
**What this suggests for Colombia (inference).**
1. *Cohort size.* The Ontario result shows that pushing more graduates into the same entry window lowers early outcomes. If Colombia's grade-11 graduates (often 16–17) all compete for the same rationed SENA and public-university places plus a thin formal youth labour market, crowding at entry is plausible. But the Ontario shock was a single extra cohort, which is not the Colombian situation.
2. *Graduation age.* The Norway and Brazil designs show that birth-date cutoffs produce sharp variation in the *age at graduation* within one cohort. In Colombia, the school-entry cutoff (age 5 for transición by a given date) should produce a within-cohort discontinuity: some graduates leave school at 16 and others at 17 or 18.
   - This gives a direct test of the "waiting room" hypothesis. If NINI incidence right after graduation is higher for children born just after the cutoff, who graduate younger, *conditional on graduating*, that supports an age-gate mechanism over a skills mechanism.
   - The main threat to this design is redshirting and grade retention.
3. *Mandated activity.* The crime-incapacitation evidence and the mixed RPA results suggest that requiring activity until 18 reduces some harms. They also suggest it does not automatically remove idleness when suitable programmes are missing. England's FFT/Wolf confound shows that supply of programmes matters.
4. Colombia has no general participation requirement after grade 11. Since 2013 it has had compulsory education through grade 11 under Ley 1324/Ley 115 provisions, but I did not verify this; check the actual legal status of compulsory media.

### Gaps
- I found no causal study of the "graduation before legal adult age" margin, where students finish secondary school below the age of full labour-market access, and its link to NEET spells.
- I did not get RPA effect sizes or the final peer-reviewed LEO-based estimates.
- I could not identify the Brazil school-entry paper's authors and outlet with certainty.
- I did not get the effect sizes from the Turkey 2012 compulsory-schooling NEET paper.

## Q2. Do age-based labour-market rules create discontinuities in youth employment and NEET? (youth minimum wages, minimum working age, child-labour laws)

### Takeaway
Age-specific minimum-wage jumps produce large, well-identified employment drops exactly at the birthday when the wage floor rises. In Denmark employment falls by 33% at 18. In Turkey the abolition of the under-16 minimum wage moved some youth into NEET in the short run.

Minimum-working-age bans, as in Brazil's 1998 rule, have small average effects. They bind only where enforcement is strong.

### Cited Findings
**Denmark: wage jump at 18**
- Kreiner, C. T., Reck, D. & Skov, P. E. (2020), "Do Lower Minimum Wages for Young Workers Raise Their Employment? Evidence from a Danish Discontinuity," *Review of Economics and Statistics* (CEPR DP 12539, 2017; CEBI WP 05-18).
- Design: RD at age 18 using monthly payroll data for the whole Danish population.
- Results:
  - The hourly wage rises 40% at 18.
  - Employment falls 33% and hours fall 45%. The aggregate wage bill is roughly unchanged.
  - The fall is driven by **job separations at 18**, not by fewer hires. Hiring slows slightly as workers approach 18.
  - The implied elasticity of youth employment to the youth minimum wage is 0.6–1.1.
- Sources: [LSE eprint (REStat version)](https://eprints.lse.ac.uk/100800/1/rest_a_00825.pdf); [CEBI WP](https://www.econ.ku.dk/cebi/publikationer/working-papers/CEBI-WP-05-18.pdf); [EconPapers CEPR DP](https://econpapers.repec.org/paper/cprceprdp/12539.htm)

**Turkey: abolition of the age-specific minimum wage**
- Dayıoğlu Tayfur, M., Küçükbayrak, M. & Tumen, S. (2022), "The impact of age-specific minimum wages on youth employment and education: a regression discontinuity analysis," *International Journal of Manpower* 43(6): 1352–1377 (IZA DP 13982; GLO DP 973).
- Design: Turkey removed the lower minimum wage for under-16s on 1 January 2014. The paper uses RD at age 16 plus difference-in-discontinuities.
- Results:
  - Employment of young men falls 2.5–3.1 percentage points and unemployment rises about 2 pp.
  - High-school enrolment does not change at first, but transitions into **NEET rise noticeably**.
  - In the medium term the NEET effect is transitory: enrolment rises and absorbs the employment loss.
  - The authors attribute the effects mainly to demand-side forces.
- Sources: [EconPapers IZA DP](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:iza:izadps:dp13982); [METU record](https://avesis.metu.edu.tr/yayin/a14e4791-09a7-4e00-bd98-9fba3c3aeaf5/the-impact-of-age-specific-minimum-wages-on-youth-employment-and-education-a-regression-discontinuity-analysis); [GLO DP](https://econpapers.repec.org/paper/zbwglodps/973.htm)

**UK: age bands in the National Minimum Wage**
- An IFS report for the Low Pay Commission compares people just before and just after age-band birthdays. It notes that young people may stay in education longer to avoid becoming NEET. — [IFS/LPC report](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/453695/1/IFS_LPC_report_copy_edited_final.pdf)
- I did not get the effect sizes.

**Brazil: minimum working age**
- Bargain, O. & Boutin, D. (2021), "Minimum Age Regulation and Child Labor: New Evidence from Brazil," *World Bank Economic Review* 35(1): 234–260.
- Design: The 1998 constitutional amendment raised the minimum working age from 14 to 16, applying only to children who turned 14 after mid-December 1998. The paper uses RD and difference-in-discontinuities.
- Results:
  - No overall effect on child labour.
  - No shift into less visible work and no substitution within families.
  - Child labour falls significantly in *visible* activities and in regions with above-average labour-inspection intensity.
- Sources: [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:wbecrv:v:35:y:2021:i:1:p:234-260.); [World Bank OKR](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/handle/10986/40293)
- Not verified: Piza & Souza and others who find that the same Brazilian ban reduced boys' employment and raised schooling. Check this before citing it as a contrasting result.

**Not verified**
- Dutch youth minimum-wage age steps (Kabátek and others), which show employment discontinuities at birthdays.
- US child-labour-law history papers.
- Direct survey evidence on employers' reluctance to hire under-18s, such as liability and paperwork costs. I found no causal study that isolates **employer reluctance to hire minors** separately from wage floors.

### Inferences
**What this suggests for Colombia (inference).**
1. *Wages are not the age-18 jump.* As far as I recall (not verified), Colombia's monthly minimum wage (SMMLV) does not vary by age, and apprentices under SENA's *contrato de aprendizaje* receive a fraction of it. So the Danish mechanism, a wage jump at 18, is probably *not* what drives Colombia's jump at 18. Colombia's age-based rules mostly sit on the other side: work below 18 needs a permit (labour-inspector or Ministry authorisation for 15–17-year-olds), working hours are restricted, and night or hazardous work is banned.
2. *The constraint lifts at 18.* The Danish and Turkish evidence shows that firms respond sharply to age-specific *costs*, and that the margin moves exactly at the birthday. By analogy, the regulatory and administrative cost of hiring a 16–17-year-old in Colombia (permit, restrictions, liability) is a cost wedge that should disappear at 18.
   - If this matters, formal employment *entry* should jump at 18. That is the mirror image of Denmark, where it was separations.
   - It can be tested with PILA (social-security) records or GEIH microdata by month of age.
3. *Enforcement shapes the response.* The Brazil result means a work ban for under-18s will bind mainly in the formal and inspected sector. Colombian minors who want to work may simply move into informal work, so recorded informal employment may not show a "waiting room" but formal employment would.
4. *The 18th birthday bundles several changes.* The cédula, the end of the work-permit requirement and the definition of military status all arrive at 18. One RD at 18 will capture all of them together. Separating them requires the variation discussed in Q3 (military, men only) and comparisons between the formal and informal sectors.

### Gaps
- I found no study on *employer demand for minors* as a distinct barrier, beyond wage floors.
- I found no study of the Colombian work-permit regime for 15–17-year-olds or how hard the permit is to obtain in practice.
- I did not verify the Colombian apprenticeship wage rules or whether any age-differentiated wage exists.

## Q3. How do military conscription and its timing or abolition affect young men's education and employment transitions?

### Takeaway
Regression-discontinuity studies of conscription abolition or exemption (France, UK, Germany) consistently find that exemption raises schooling. They mostly find no or small effects on later employment and earnings.

In Colombia, the relevant channel is probably not service itself. It is more likely the *libreta militar* (proof of resolved military status) as a requirement for formal hiring, and the uncertainty of being drafted at 18. Evidence on that channel is thin, and the one difference-in-differences study I found reports a null.

### Cited Findings
**France**
- Mouganie, P. (2020), "Conscription and the Returns to Education: Evidence from a Regression Discontinuity," *Scandinavian Journal of Economics* 122(3): 1112–1139.
- Design: RD on the 1997 abolition of French conscription, by birth cohort.
- Results: Exemption increases schooling but has no detectable effect on employment or wages at ages 30–36. The author reads this as a low marginal return to the conscription-induced schooling.
- Sources: [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:scandj:v:122:y:2020:i:3:p:1112-1139); [MPRA WP](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/62508/)

**UK**
- Buonanno, P. & Leonida, L. (working paper, University of Bergamo, 2006), using the 1960 abolition of UK National Service as a discontinuity.
- Result: Exemption raised the probability that young men were enrolled in school at 18 or older by about 2–4 pp.
- I could not confirm the earnings estimates. — [Univ. Bergamo WP](https://aisberg.unibg.it/retrieve/e40f7b84-023a-afca-e053-6605fe0aeaf2/WPEco04%282006%29Buonanno.pdf)

**Germany**
- Bender, Paloyo & Schmidt find some evidence that conscription raised completion of higher education.
- Keller, Poutvaara & Wagner (2009), on the 1955 German draft, find no significant effect on lifetime earnings, wages or days employed. Their RD estimates are imprecise.
- Both are cited in a CReAM discussion paper. — [CReAM CDP 17/21](https://www.cream-migration.org/publ_uploads/CDP_17_21.pdf)

**Colombia: the libreta militar**
- Ley 1780 de 2016 (Ley ProJoven) barred employers from requiring the libreta militar to hire.
- A Javeriana master's thesis (Felipe Torres, 2020) uses difference-in-differences and finds no employment effect. The one marginal result is significant only at 10% and not robust. — [Javeriana repository](https://apidspace.javeriana.edu.co/server/api/core/bitstreams/7917b8f9-55ab-490c-9c7a-8a807af8d75d/content)
- Congressional bills from 2015 onward describe resolving military status as a barrier to youth labour-market access. — [PL 2015, Gaceta 880](https://cdn.actualicese.com/normatividad/2015/Proyectos/PL002-15c.pdf); [Gaceta 1064 de 2024](https://sidn.ramajudicial.gov.co/SIDN/PUBLICACIONES%20PERIODICAS/TEXTO%20COMPLETO%20Y%20TABLAS%20DE%20CONTENIDO/GACETA%20DEL%20CONGRESO/GACETA%20DEL%20CONGRESO%202024/GC%201064%20DE%202024.PDF)
- A 2022 bill to bar employers and the public sector from requiring the libreta passed its first debate in committee. — [Noticias RCN](https://www.noticiasrcn.com/colombia/a-punto-de-eliminarse-la-libreta-militar-para-trabajar-430826)
- I could not confirm whether it became law, or the current legal status.

**Not verified**
- Card & Cardoso (2012) on Portuguese conscription.
- Bauer et al. on German conscription and wages.
- Angrist's Vietnam draft-lottery papers, which are well known but not searched here.
- Galiani, Rossi & Schargrodsky (2011, AEJ: Applied), "Conscription and Crime," which uses the Argentine draft lottery and finds that conscription *raised* later crime. This is relevant to LAC, but I did not verify it in this session.

### Inferences
**What this suggests for Colombia (inference).**
1. *Draft risk as a waiting room.* Men who finish grade 11 at 16–17 face uncertain draft status at 18. Rational employers and young men may postpone formal hiring or training commitments until military status is resolved. This is a *male-specific* waiting-room channel.
2. *A built-in test.* If the age-18 NINI jump is concentrated among men and among those without resolved status, that supports this channel. If it is equally large for women, the binding constraint is something else, such as age or permit rules or admission rationing.
3. *A second test.* The gap between men and women in NINI rates around 17–19, before and after Ley 1780/2016, gives a feasible difference-in-differences (or difference-in-discontinuities) design.
4. *What the international evidence implies.* The education effects come mainly from the timing and opportunity cost of service. Deferment rules tied to enrolment (Colombian bachilleres have historically had deferment and education-linked options, though I did not verify this) could therefore steer enrolment choices at 18.

### Gaps
- I found no causal study of Colombian military service, its exemptions or deferments, or the libreta requirement on NINI status.
- I could not verify the current legal status of the libreta requirement after 2022–2024.

## Q4. What happens to rejected applicants when tertiary places are rationed (gap years, delayed enrolment)?

### Takeaway
Admission-cutoff RD designs are standard in Chile, Colombia and Scandinavia. They show that crossing a cutoff changes enrolment and earnings. However, the papers I found report results for admitted versus rejected applicants and say little about what rejected applicants actually do in between, such as idleness, re-applying, private enrolment or work.

Swedish evidence shows that delaying higher education carries a lasting earnings cost, mostly because of lost experience.

### Cited Findings
**Sweden: gap years**
- Holmlund, B., Liu, Q. & Nordström Skans, O. (2008), "Mind the gap? Estimating the effects of postponing higher education," *Oxford Economic Papers* 60(4): 683–710 (IFAU WP 2006:11; CESifo WP 1792).
- Design: Swedish administrative data.
- Results: Postponing university leaves a persistent earnings shortfall, mostly because of fewer post-graduation years of experience. A two-year delay costs roughly 40–50% of annual age-40 earnings in lifetime present value.
- Identification relies on selection-on-observables and IV-type arguments; I did not verify the details.
- Sources: [IFAU](https://www.ifau.se/en/Research/Publications/Working-papers/2006/Mind-the-gap-Estimating-the-effects-of-postponing-higher-education/); [CESifo](https://www.cesifo.org/en/publications/2006/working-paper/mind-gap-estimating-effects-postponing-higher-education)

**Chile: admission cutoffs**
- Hastings, J., Neilson, C. & Zimmerman, S. (2013), "Are Some Degrees Worth More than Others? Evidence from college admission cutoffs in Chile," NBER WP 19241.
- Design: Admission thresholds at more than 1,100 programmes, covering 30 cohorts, linked to tax records.
- Results: Returns are large and positive for selective degrees and for health, science and social science. The paper reports admission effects and does not separately describe the outside option of rejected applicants.
- Source: [NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w19241)

**Colombia: university admission cutoffs**
- Studies of Universidad Nacional, Los Andes and Univalle exist.
  - Los Andes: an admission offer raises enrolment by 13.8 pp. — [Saavedra-type paper, "The Returns to College Quality," Uniandes seminar](https://Economia.Uniandes.Edu.co/sites/default/files/seminariocede/The-Returns-to-College-Quality.pdf)
  - Univalle: tracking at the margin. — [de Roux & Riehl](https://evanriehl.github.io/papers/drr_college_tracking_mar2022.pdf)
  - Universidad Nacional: an admission RD finds effects on access and wages. Search output gave no outlet or link; possibly [Economics of Education Review 70 (2019), pp. 20–34](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/ecoedu/v70y2019icp20-34.html), which I did not confirm.
- None of the snippets reports the NEET or idleness rate of rejected applicants.

**Colombia: SENA admission**
- A preliminary paper uses RDs at the **SENA entry exam** and at the Jóvenes en Acción cutoff to study liquidity constraints and post-secondary enrolment. It is marked "do not cite." — [CORE PDF](https://core.ac.uk/download/288500327.pdf)
- Mancino, Morales & Salazar (2023 WP) use a sharp RD at SENA *certification* thresholds, with PILA records. They find signalling effects on moves from self-employment to salaried work and no effect for the unemployed. — [ESEM 2023 paper](https://congress-files.s3.amazonaws.com/2023-08/signaling_in_colombia_august2023.pdf)

**Not verified**
- Kirkeboen, Leuven & Mogstad (2016, QJE) on field-of-study admission cutoffs in Norway.
- Öckert (2010, Economics of Education Review) on Swedish university admission and what rejected applicants do; I recall that it finds rejected applicants re-apply and that the cost is mainly delay.
- Londoño-Vélez, Rodríguez & Sánchez (2020, AEJ: Policy) on *Ser Pilo Paga*. A related multi-score fuzzy-RD paper appears on [CLEF WP 064 (2023)](https://clef.uwaterloo.ca/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/CLEF-064-2023.pdf).

### Inferences
**What this suggests for Colombia (inference).**
1. *Rationing creates waiting.* Public universities admit by exam each semester and SENA rations places, so a young graduate who misses a cutoff faces a waiting period that can be measured. The Swedish gap-year evidence implies such delays are costly, mainly through lost experience.
2. *The existing data already contain the answer.* The Colombian cutoff studies (UNAL, Univalle, SENA) contain exactly the rejected-applicant samples needed. Linking them to PILA formal-employment records, SPADIES/SNIES re-enrolment and ICFES records would show directly whether rejected applicants just below a cutoff are more likely to be NINI 6–18 months later, and whether the effect is larger for applicants under 18.
3. *Admission rationing interacts with the age gate.* A rejected 16-year-old cannot fall back on formal work. A rejected 19-year-old can. This age heterogeneity is a sharp test of the waiting-room hypothesis.

### Gaps
- I found no published causal estimate of the NEET or idleness outcome for rejected applicants in Colombia or Chile.
- I did not verify the Öckert or Kirkeboen et al. details.
- The SENA-entry-exam RD paper is a preliminary draft.

## Q5. How do vocational tracks and apprenticeships compare with general education on youth NEET and unemployment?

### Takeaway
Vocational and apprenticeship systems improve *early* employment. Hanushek et al. find that this edge may fade later in life. RCTs in developing countries (Colombia's Jóvenes en Acción, Uganda, Côte d'Ivoire) show that pre-entry vocational training with certifiable skills and firm-based apprenticeships raise formal earnings and employment. The gains appear with lags, and they come from training *after* age 18.

### Cited Findings
**Across countries: general vs vocational education over the life cycle**
- Hanushek, E., Schwerdt, G., Woessmann, L. & Zhang, L. (2017), "General Education, Vocational Education, and Labor-Market Outcomes over the Lifecycle," *Journal of Human Resources* 52(1): 48–87.
- Design: IALS microdata for 11 countries, using a difference-in-differences across ages that compares people with general and vocational education. Robustness checks use the German Microcensus (within occupation) and Austrian plant closures.
- Results: Vocational education gives an employment advantage when young that is offset by lower employment later in life. The trade-off is strongest in apprenticeship-intensive countries.
- Sources: [JHR](https://jhr.uwpress.org/content/52/1/48); [Hanushek page](https://hanushek.stanford.edu/publications/general-education-vocational-education-and-labor-market-outcomes-over-life-cycle)

**Colombia: Jóvenes en Acción vouchers (RCT)**
- Short run: Attanasio, Kugler & Meghir (2011, *AEJ: Applied*). The programme served unemployed 18–25-year-olds in the poorest income quintile. Training centres selected trainees from among applicants, which created the randomisation. Women's earnings rose about 19.6%, with no detectable effect for men in the short run.
- Long run: Attanasio, Guarín, Medina & Meghir (2015), "Long Term Impacts of Vouchers for Vocational Training: Experimental Evidence for Colombia," NBER WP 21390.
  - Up to eight years later, formal earnings are about 12.4% higher for both sexes, driven by formal employment.
  - Participants are more likely to work in large formal firms.
  - The offer had no effect on secondary completion.
  - Sample sizes and some percentages differ across J-PAL pages, so verify them against the paper.
- Sources: [J-PAL evaluation summary](https://povertyactionlab.org/node/1668)

**Uganda: vocational training vs firm-provided training (RCT)**
- Alfonsi, L., Bandiera, O., Bassi, V., Burgess, R., Rasul, I., Sulaiman, M. & Vitali, A. (2020), "Tackling Youth Unemployment: Evidence From a Labor Market Experiment in Uganda," *Econometrica* 88(6): 2369–2414.
- Design: About 1,700 workers and 1,500 firms followed over four years. The arms compare six months of sector-specific vocational training (VT) with wage subsidies to firms that train workers (FT).
- Results:
  - Both raise employment. VT gains are larger and last longer; FT gains arrive fast but fade.
  - A secondary summary reports employment +21% (VT) vs +14% (FT) and earnings +34% vs +20%. These numbers are not confirmed against the paper.
  - The mechanism is certifiable skills that generate more job offers.
- Sources: [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wly:emetrp:v:88:y:2020:i:6:p:2369-2414); [UK gov R4D page](https://www.gov.uk/research-for-development-outputs/tackling-youth-unemployment-evidence-from-a-labor-market-experiment-in-uganda)

**Côte d'Ivoire: subsidised dual apprenticeships (RCT)**
- Crépon, B. & Premand, P. Applicants aged 18–24 were randomised.
  - At 20 months there was no effect on average earnings; lost wage and self-employment income was offset by apprenticeship pay and allowances.
  - The intervention created 0.74–0.77 new positions per subsidised apprentice, with no crowd-out (IZA DP 12793, 2019).
  - A 2026 World Bank WP with seven years of follow-up finds earnings up 14–20% two to five years after completion, through self-employment, with no effect on wage employment.
- Sources: [IZA DP 12793](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:iza:izadps:dp12793); [World Bank WP 11312](https://econpapers.repec.org/paper/wbkwbrwps/11312.htm)

**Colombia: SENA training and certification (non-experimental or RD)**
- Matching estimates (Medina & Núñez) find no significant earnings effect for youths. — [CORE](https://core.ac.uk/works/2587906)
- Jiménez, Kugler & Horn find that correcting for selection shrinks SENA's estimated effects. — [searchworks record](https://searchworks.jioinstitute.edu.in/articles/edsggo__edsgcl.4826713)

**Not verified**
- Krueger & Pischke and Ryan (2001, JEL) on the dual system and the school-to-work transition.
- Hicks, Kremer, Mbiti & Miguel's Kenya vocational-voucher RCT.
- Card, Kluve & Weber meta-analyses of active labour-market programmes.
- Kluve et al. (2019, World Development) meta-analysis of youth employment interventions.
- Eichhorst et al. (2015) on vocational education and training worldwide.
- Cross-country evidence that dual-system countries (Germany, Switzerland, Austria) have low youth unemployment and NEET is mostly descriptive.

### Inferences
**What this suggests for Colombia (inference).**
1. *All the positive programmes start at 18.* The Colombian evidence (JeA) and the Ivorian and Ugandan evidence all start at age 18 or older. None of them covers the 16–17 gap.
2. *Colombia's media técnica might help, at a price.* Colombia's vocational upper secondary (*media técnica*, often with SENA articulation) could in principle give 16–17-year-olds a certifiable credential. The Hanushek et al. trade-off implies it would help early employment at some long-run adaptability cost.
3. *Credentials do not help if 16–17-year-olds cannot be hired.* If graduates under 18 cannot be formally hired regardless of credentials, media técnica will show *no* NINI advantage before 18 but an advantage after 18. That pattern would be diagnostic of an age gate.
4. *The contrato de aprendizaje.* SENA's apprenticeship contract (quota-based obligation on firms; I did not verify the details) is the natural institutional channel for minors. Its age eligibility and take-up by under-18s are worth documenting.

### Gaps
- I found no RCT of apprenticeships specifically for minors aged 15–17 in a developing country.
- I found no causal study comparing Colombian *media técnica* with *media académica* graduates on NINI status by age.

## Q6. What does Latin American evidence show about the timing of the school-to-work transition and NEET spikes?

### Takeaway
Regional diagnostics put about one in five Latin American youths aged 15–24 out of school and work, concentrated among poor households and women. The growth of the NEET population has been driven by men.

Colombian evidence confirms a sharp rise in NINI rates between ages 16 and 18, and that NINI spells in this window predict worse labour outcomes decades later. These are descriptive studies, not causal ones. I found no LAC causal study tied to specific transition institutions at that age.

### Cited Findings
**Latin America (descriptive)**
- de Hoyos, R., Rogers, H. & Székely, M. (2016), *Out of School and Out of Work: Risk and Opportunities for Latin America's Ninis*, World Bank.
  - About 1 in 5 youths aged 15–24 is a nini.
  - About 60% of ninis come from the bottom 40% of the income distribution, and 66% are women.
  - Over two decades the number of ninis grew by about 2 million, driven by men.
  - Sources: [World Bank OKR](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/entities/publication/83e4ac1b-aa2f-5eb2-ad54-db1acdbb5200/full)
- Background paper: de Hoyos, Popova & Rogers (2016), WPS 7548, using household surveys from 1992–2013. — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wbk:wbrwps:7548)
- The regional report also links early school dropout and teenage pregnancy to nini status, and links ninis to crime and violence in contexts like Colombia, Mexico and Central America. I recall this but did not verify the specific claims.

**Colombia (descriptive, synthetic-cohort panel)**
- Lasso-Valderrama, F. J. & Vargas-Riaño, C. O., "Jóvenes que ni estudian ni trabajan: Dinámica y determinantes en Colombia," Borradores de Economía No. 1363, Banco de la República. The bank's page lists a 2026 date.
  - Design: synthetic-cohort panel from household surveys, 1984–2024.
  - Result: a strong acceleration in the NINI rate between 16 and 18. Press coverage cites the risk rising from 5% to 22% over that range.
  - NINI status is highly persistent. Being NINI at 16–22 is associated with lower employment and higher unemployment about 20 years later.
  - These results are descriptive and associational.
  - Sources: [Banrep page](https://banrep.gov.co/es/publicaciones-investigaciones/borradores-economia/jovenes-estudian-trabajan-dinamica); [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/p/bdr/borrec/1363.html); [Portafolio coverage](https://www.portafolio.co/economia/los-nini-frenan-la-productividad-a-largo-plazo-y-el-pib-potencial-del-pais-advierte-el-banco-de-la-republica-502316)
- A local Cartagena study finds that most NINIs' highest completed level is *media académica* (completed secondary). — [Cartagena planning office PDF](https://planeacion.cartagena.gov.co/sites/default/files/2025-12/04nini.pdf)

**Brazil**
- The minimum-working-age RD (Bargain & Boutin 2021, Q2) and the school-entry RD (Q1) are the closest LAC causal evidence on transition rules.

### Inferences
**What this suggests for Colombia (inference).**
1. *The jump is timed with the end of school.* The 16–18 jump in Colombia lines up with the end of an 11-grade school system and with turning 18. The regional pattern of ninis being disproportionately completed-secondary, poor and female suggests both an institutional gap after graduation and household-specific factors (care work, early motherhood).
2. *Gender is diagnostic.* A waiting-room mechanism predicts a NINI spell that *ends* at 18–19 for those who then find work or study. Care and motherhood channels predict a persistent spell concentrated among women.
3. *Decomposing the spike.* Because NINI status is persistent in the Banrep work, the Colombian spike is not purely transitory. A decomposition by graduation age and sex would separate these channels.

### Gaps
- I found no causal LAC study linking a specific transition institution (graduation age, permit regime, military status, admission rationing) to a NEET spike at 16–18.
- I could not obtain single-year-of-age NINI rates by graduation status from the snippets; this needs direct GEIH microdata analysis.

## Q7. Synthesis: which mechanisms have strong evidence, which are plausible for Colombia, and how could they be tested?

### Takeaway
The strongest international causal evidence shows two things. Employers respond sharply to **age-specific labour costs or rules at the exact birthday** (Denmark, Turkey). And **conscription timing and admission rationing change the timing of education**, with lasting costs of delay (France/UK conscription; Sweden gap years).

For Colombia, an "age-18 waiting room" is plausible but untested. Its most likely components are the end of the permit regime at 18, cédula-based formal contracting, and military-status resolution for men, all interacting with rationed SENA and university places. Colombia's 11-grade system and its school-entry cutoffs supply natural experiments to test it.

### Cited Findings
**Strong causal evidence (well-identified, published)**
- Age-specific wage floors move youth employment sharply at the birthday. — [Kreiner, Reck & Skov (REStat 2020)](https://eprints.lse.ac.uk/100800/1/rest_a_00825.pdf); [Dayıoğlu Tayfur et al. (IJM 2022)](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:iza:izadps:dp13982)
- Compulsory schooling and dropout-age laws reduce youth crime, an "incapacitation" effect of mandated activity. — [Anderson (REStat 2014)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/tpr/restat/v96y2014i2p318-331.html); [Machin, Marie & Vujić (EJ 2011)](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/28727)
- Conscription exemption raises schooling, with small or null later labour effects. — [Mouganie (SJE 2020)](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:scandj:v:122:y:2020:i:3:p:1112-1139)
- Vocational training with certifiable skills raises youth employment and formal earnings in developing countries. — [Alfonsi et al. (Econometrica 2020)](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wly:emetrp:v:88:y:2020:i:6:p:2369-2414); [J-PAL JeA](https://povertyactionlab.org/node/1668)

**Moderate evidence**
- Larger entry cohorts lower early earnings. — [Morin (LE 2015)](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:labeco:v:32:y:2015:i:c:p:99-111)
- Delaying higher education has persistent earnings costs. — [Holmlund, Liu & Nordström Skans (OEP 2008)](https://www.ifau.se/en/Research/Publications/Working-papers/2006/Mind-the-gap-Estimating-the-effects-of-postponing-higher-education/)
- Vocational education trades early employment for later adaptability. — [Hanushek et al. (JHR 2017)](https://jhr.uwpress.org/content/52/1/48)

**Weak or mixed evidence**
- Raising the participation age to 18 had mixed NEET effects. — [Bath/IPR](https://www.bath.ac.uk/announcements/policy-to-keep-teens-in-education-until-18-has-limited-impact-study-finds/); [FFT](https://ffteducationdatalab.org.uk/2025/07/did-raising-the-participation-age-lead-to-increased-post-16-participation/)
- Minimum-working-age bans have small average effects that depend on enforcement. — [Bargain & Boutin (WBER 2021)](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:wbecrv:v:35:y:2021:i:1:p:234-260.)
- Removing the libreta militar hiring requirement in Colombia had a null result in a master's thesis. — [Javeriana thesis](https://apidspace.javeriana.edu.co/server/api/core/bitstreams/7917b8f9-55ab-490c-9c7a-8a807af8d75d/content)

### Inferences
**Mechanisms plausible for Colombia, and how to test them with Colombian data (inference).**

1. **Age gate at 18: permits, cédula and contracting (all youths).**
   - Prediction: formal-employment *entry* jumps at the 18th birthday, and the NINI rate falls right after 18 among graduates who were NINI at 17.
   - Test: an RD in exact age (birth month) using GEIH, which gives age in years plus survey month; birth month may need ECV or other sources. A better source is PILA/administrative formal-employment records, which have date of birth.
   - Placebo: there should be no jump at 18 for youths still enrolled in school.

2. **Graduating young (the timing of an 11-grade system).**
   - Prediction: conditional on graduating, children born just after the school-entry cutoff graduate older and should have *lower* post-graduation NINI duration if the age gate binds.
   - Test: a school-entry-cutoff RD (Black–Devereux–Salvanes or Brazil style) using SIMAT enrolment records linked to Saber 11 (ICFES) and to PILA/SPADIES. A complementary test compares graduates of the same Saber 11 cohort who differ in age at the exam.
   - Watch-outs: grade retention and redshirting; check density and first-stage strength.

3. **Military-status uncertainty (men).**
   - Prediction: the NINI jump around 18 is larger for men, and larger before Ley 1780/2016 (ProJoven) than after.
   - Test: a difference-in-discontinuities at age 18 by sex, before and after 2016, using GEIH. Any data on libreta possession would allow heterogeneity analysis. Analogy: Mouganie's and Bargain–Boutin's difference-in-discontinuity designs.

4. **Rationed post-secondary places (waiting lists and re-application).**
   - Prediction: applicants just below SENA or public-university cutoffs have higher NINI and idleness 6–18 months later, especially those under 18 who cannot fall back on formal work.
   - Test: admission-cutoff RD using SENA application and entry-exam records or UNAL/Univalle admissions, linked to SPADIES/SNIES (re-enrolment) and PILA (employment). Compare outcomes by applicant age (under 18 vs 18 and over).

5. **Vocational credential (media técnica / SENA articulation).**
   - Prediction: if the age gate binds, media técnica graduates show no NINI advantage at 16–17 but an advantage after 18.
   - Test: a difference-in-differences or event study around school-level adoption of media técnica or SENA articulation programmes, using SIMAT plus PILA. Use age profiles of NINI by track in GEIH as a descriptive first pass.

6. **Wage floor at 18.**
   - This is probably *not* operative, because Colombia's minimum wage is not age-differentiated (not verified). The Danish separations-at-18 mechanism should therefore be absent.
   - Finding no jump in *separations* at 18 in PILA data would help rule it out and isolate the entry-side age gate.

**Overall assessment (inference).** The international literature strongly supports the idea that birthday-based rules create sharp employment discontinuities and that delays at transition carry lasting costs. No study directly documents a "waiting room" in which graduates under 18 are idle because legal or administrative age thresholds bind. A Colombian study combining a school-entry-cutoff RD (variation in graduation age) with an RD at the 18th birthday on administrative data would therefore be a novel contribution.

### Gaps
- There is no direct causal international evidence on the "graduated-before-18" margin.
- Many effect sizes are taken from abstracts or snippets and need full-text checks.
- These items are recalled but **not verified** and should be checked before citing:
  - Öckert (2010)
  - Kirkeboen, Leuven & Mogstad (2016)
  - Fredriksson & Öckert (2014)
  - Galiani, Rossi & Schargrodsky (2011)
  - Hicks et al. (Kenya)
  - Kluve et al. (2019)
  - Piza & Souza (Brazil)
  - The Dutch youth-minimum-wage RDs
  - Ryan (2001)

  - The exact work-permit rules for 15–17-year-olds
  - Apprenticeship wage rates
  - Whether media is formally compulsory
  - The current status of the libreta militar requirement
