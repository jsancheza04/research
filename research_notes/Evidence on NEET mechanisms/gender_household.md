## Q1. Do teen/early childbearing and early marriage causally reduce young women's schooling and employment?

### Takeaway
Credible designs (menarche IV, miscarriage IV, matching) consistently find that early motherhood or marriage **reduces schooling**. The **labor-supply** effects are much smaller or disappear once selection is handled, including in the only Colombian causal study (Agüero, menarche IV). Early **marriage**, by contrast, sharply reduces market work in settings where private wage work is hard to combine with domestic roles (MENA).

### Cited Findings
- **Agüero (CAF working paper, 2021), "Misallocated Talent: Teen Pregnancy, Education and Job Sorting in Colombia."**
  - *Setting and design:* Colombia, with age at menarche as the instrument for teen motherhood. **Causal (IV).**
  - *Schooling and fertility:* Teen pregnancy reduces school attainment and raises the number of children ever born.
  - *Labor:* Across eight labor indicators (participation, job type, occupation), corrected for multiple testing, the effects are little to none. Much, if not all, of the apparent negative labor effect is selection.
  - *Mechanism:* The paper discusses family networks and co-residence as buffers.
  - *Sources:* [CAF Scioteca](https://scioteca.caf.com/handle/123456789/1727); [IDEAS listing](https://ideas.repec.org/p/dbl/dblwop/1727.html); [Banrep seminar page](https://www.banrep.gov.co/es/seminario-microeconomia-aplicada-misallocated-talent-teen-pregnancy-education-and-job-sorting). Full text not read.
- **Field & Ambrus (2008), "Early Marriage, Age of Menarche, and Female Schooling Attainment in Bangladesh," *Journal of Political Economy* 116(5).**
  - *Setting and design:* Rural Bangladesh, with menarche as the IV for marriage timing. **Causal (IV).**
  - *Effects:* Each extra year of marriage delay brings about 0.22 more years of schooling and 5.6% higher literacy, plus more use of preventive health services.
  - *Policy simulation:* The authors simulate age-of-consent laws in a marriage-market equilibrium.
  - *Page range:* Indexes disagree (881–891 vs 881–930); the DOI is 10.1086/593333.
  - *Sources:* [Harvard DASH PDF](https://dash.harvard.edu/bitstream/1/3200264/2/ambrus_earlymarriage.pdf); [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:ucp:jpolec:v:116:y:2008:i:5:p:881-930).
- **Assaad, Krafft & Selwaness (2022), "The impact of early marriage on women's employment in the Middle East and North Africa," *Feminist Economics* 28(2): 247–279** (ERF working paper 2017).
  - *Effect on market work:* Marrying by the median age cuts women's probability of market work by about 47% in Jordan, 30% in Tunisia (33% in the working paper) and 16% in Egypt.
  - *Private wage work:* Most of the drop is in private wage work. The working paper puts the fall at about 76% (Jordan), 57% (Tunisia) and 40% (Egypt).
  - *Design:* The design is quasi-causal and is said to treat marriage timing as endogenous. The exact identification was **not verified from snippets**.
  - *Sources:* [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/femeco/v28y2022i2p247-279.html); [ERF WP listing](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:erg:wpaper:1086).
- **Hotz, McElroy & Sanders (2005), "Teenage Childbearing and Its Life Cycle Consequences: Exploiting a Natural Experiment," *Journal of Human Resources* 40(3).**
  - *Setting and design:* US (NLSY79), with miscarriage as the IV. **Causal (IV).**
  - *Effects:* Adverse effects right after the first birth are short-lived. Teen childbearing even appears to raise later labor supply and earnings, so most of the apparent cost reflects pre-existing disadvantage.
  - *Sources:* [EconPapers JHR](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:uwp:jhriss:v:40:y:2005:i:2:p683-715); [NBER w7397](https://www.nber.org/papers/w7397.pdf).
- **Arceo-Gómez & Campos-Vázquez (2014), "Teenage Pregnancy in Mexico: Evolution and Consequences," *Latin American Journal of Economics* 51(1).**
  - *Design:* Propensity-score matching. **Not a strong causal design.**
  - *Long-run effects:* About 1–1.2 fewer years of schooling and lower household income per capita.
  - *Earlier version:* Reported 0.6–0.8 fewer years, lower attendance, fewer hours worked and a higher marriage rate.
  - *Sources:* [Revistas Chilenas](https://revistaschilenas.uchile.cl/handle/2250/61312); [Colmex DT 2012-3](https://cee.colmex.mx/dts/2012/DT-2012-3.pdf).
- **Kruger & Berthelon (2011), "Risky behavior among youth: Incapacitation effects of school on adolescent motherhood and crime in Chile," *Journal of Public Economics*** (IZA DP 4553, 2009).
  - *Design:* The staggered rollout of Chile's full-day school reform is the source of variation. **Quasi-experimental.**
  - *Effects:* A 20% increase in full-day municipal enrollment reduces the likelihood of teen motherhood by 5%. Effects are concentrated among poor and urban families.
  - *Sources:* [IZA DP 4553](https://docs.iza.org/dp4553.pdf); [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:iza:izadps:dp4553).
- **Jensen (2012), "Do Labor Market Opportunities Affect Young Women's Work and Family Decisions? Experimental Evidence from India," *QJE* 127(2).**
  - *Design:* Randomized BPO recruiting services in villages. **RCT.**
  - *Effects for women aged 15–21:* +4.6 pp working in BPO jobs, +2.4 pp working for pay outside the home, about 5.1 pp less likely to marry or give birth, more schooling, and a desired 0.35 fewer children.
  - *Note:* The age bands differ across summaries.
  - *Sources:* [J-PAL](https://povertyactionlab.org/media/3810); [Harvard GAP](https://www.gap.hks.harvard.edu/do-labor-market-opportunities-affect-young-women%E2%80%99s-work-and-family-decisions-experimental-evidence).
- **Ashcraft, Fernández-Val & Lang (2013, *Economic Journal*):** a miscarriage-IV refinement that corrects for non-random miscarriage. **(not verified)**. The search did not return it.

### Inferences
- **Colombia:** Agüero's IV result suggests that in Colombia early motherhood mainly harms **schooling**, not labor supply. Female NINI status after early motherhood may therefore be driven partly by **selection** (pre-existing disadvantage) and partly by lost schooling, rather than by a large direct effect of motherhood on work. Co-residence with extended kin, which Agüero flags as a buffer, may be what keeps labor effects small. Ties to Q3: if grandmothers or older daughters absorb the care, the cost moves to them.
- **Colombia:** The MENA evidence points to **unions** (cohabitation, common in Colombia) as a separate margin from births. Because unions shift women out of formal private wage work in particular, unions in Colombia may matter more for *formal* employment than for any employment.
- **Colombia:** Chile's full-day school result suggests Colombia's **Jornada Única** rollout could be a quasi-experiment for adolescent fertility and later NINI status.
- **Colombia:** Jensen's RCT suggests that perceived returns to female jobs shift marriage and fertility timing. Expanded "gendered entry jobs" (services, BPO and call centers in Colombian cities) could delay unions and births.

### Gaps
- I found no causal Colombian estimate of **early union / cohabitation** (as opposed to births) on schooling or NINI status.
- Agüero's exact coefficients and data source (ENDS?) were not seen; the full text could not be fetched.
- I could not verify the Gitter, Onyemeziem & Corcoran (2023) and Sen Chakraborty & Villa (2024) papers. They are mentioned only as citing Agüero in a search summary.

## Q2. Does childcare availability raise young mothers' employment (LatAm focus)?

### Takeaway
LatAm quasi-experiments and RCTs mostly find **positive effects of childcare on maternal participation and employment**, with sizes ranging from modest to large. The Colombian evidence (Medellín's Buen Comienzo) shows participation effects but small employment effects. Grandmothers are a key informal childcare source (Mexico). The Rio lottery shows that childcare can free **other household members (grandparents, adolescent siblings)** rather than mothers.

### Cited Findings
- **Martínez & Perticará (2017), "Childcare effects on maternal employment: Evidence from Chile," *Journal of Development Economics*.**
  - *Program and design:* The randomized "4 a 7" after-school program for children aged 6–13. **RCT.**
  - *Effects:* Employment +5% and labor force participation +7%.
  - *Sources:* [J-PAL evaluation page](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/impact-extending-childcare-female-labor-chile); [UC Economía](https://economia.uc.cl/biblioteca/childcare-effects-on-maternal-employment-evidence-from-chile/).
- **Berlinski & Galiani (2007, *Labour Economics*; IFS WP 04/30), "The Effect of a Large Expansion of Pre-primary School Facilities on Preschool Attendance and Maternal Employment."**
  - *Setting and design:* Argentina's construction of about 175,000 preschool places (1994–2000), exploited as a quasi-experiment.
  - *Effects:* Large take-up and increased maternal employment. The magnitude was not seen in snippets.
  - *Sources:* [UCL Discovery](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/2847/); [UdeSA repository](https://repositorio.udesa.edu.ar/items/e2371510-c671-4211-a900-9a2978d8febb).
- **Attanasio, Paes de Barros, Carneiro, Evans, Lima, Olinto, Schady (2022), "Public Childcare, Labor Market Outcomes of Caregivers, and Child Development: Experimental Evidence from Brazil" (NBER w30653; IZA DP 15705; CGD WP 728).**
  - *Design:* In Rio de Janeiro in 2007, 24,000 applicants aged 0–3 were randomly assigned to 10,000 free daycare slots. **RCT (lottery).**
  - *Take-up:* Winning raised time in daycare by 34% in the early years.
  - *Grandparents:* Co-resident grandparents were 20 pp more likely to be employed four years after enrollment.
  - *Older siblings:* Adolescent siblings aged 15 and older were also more likely to be employed.
  - *Parents:* Already highly employed, and little changed by the lottery.
  - *Later follow-up:* By 2015 the grandparent and sibling effects were smaller and no longer significant.
  - *Sources:* [NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w30653); [J-PAL PDF](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/sites/default/files/research-paper/WP4285_Public-childcare-in-Brazil_Attanasio-et-all_Nov22.pdf); [CGD PDF](https://www.cgdev.org/sites/default/files/public-childcare-labor-market-outcomes-caregivers-and-child-development-experimental.pdf); [VoxDev](https://voxdev.org/node/75833).
- **Talamas (2023), "Grandmothers and the Gender Gap in the Mexican Labor Market," *Journal of Development Economics*** (IDB WP 2022).
  - *Design:* Triple difference on the timing of grandmothers' deaths. **Quasi-experimental.**
  - *Effects:* A grandmother's death lowers mothers' employment by 12 pp (about 27%) and leaves fathers unaffected. The drop persists for at least four quarters and is smaller where public daycare is available.
  - *Sources:* [IDB WP](https://publications.iadb.org/publications/english/document/Grandmothers-and-the-Gender-Gap-in-the-Mexican-Labor-Market.pdf); [IDB brief](https://publications.iadb.org/publications/english/document/Research-Insights-Can-Increased-Childcare-Availability-Shrink-the-Gender-Gap-in-Employment.pdf).
- **Calderón (Banco de México WP 2014-07), Mexico's Estancias Infantiles.**
  - *Effects:* The program raised women's probability of working and reduced their child-rearing time. Husbands also spent less time on child rearing and housework.
  - *Gap:* No outcomes for older siblings were found.
  - *Source:* [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bdm:wpaper:2014-07).
- **Cardona-Sosa & Morales (2015), "Efectos laborales de los servicios de cuidado infantil: evidencia del programa Buen Comienzo," Banco de la República Borrador 882.**
  - *Program:* Medellín's free childcare for children under 5, eight hours a day.
  - *Design:* Matching and IV. **Non-experimental / quasi-causal.**
  - *Participation:* +3 to 9 pp (10–27%), and up to +31 pp for mothers living near centers.
  - *Employment:* The effect on employment ("empleabilidad") is minimal.
  - *Sources:* [Banrep Borrador 882](https://banrep.gov.co/es/borrador-882); [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/p/col/000094/012787.html).
- **Hogares Comunitarios de Bienestar (Colombia).** The Attanasio and Vera-Hernández evaluations found by search concern child nutrition and development, not maternal employment:
  - Attanasio, Di Maro & Vera-Hernández (2013, *Economic Journal* 123): nutrition. [UCL Discovery](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/14749/1/14749.pdf)
  - Attanasio & Vera-Hernández (IFS 2004): medium- and long-run effects.
  - **I found no verified Attanasio paper estimating HCB effects on maternal employment.**
- **Peru (Wawa Wasi / Cuna Más).**
  - *Employment:* No rigorous national evaluation of maternal employment was found. A 2025 Cusco study reports falling female unemployment among users, but the abstract shows no causal design ([search summary; Universidad Andina journal](https://revistas.uandina.edu.pe/index.php/integracion/article/download/1262/611/3733)).
  - *Other outcomes:* RD and exit-rule studies examine maternal mental health ([PUCP repository](https://repositorio.pucp.edu.pe/items/80aba8cd-31a7-4280-81f8-fb4cdfc9bfd4/full)).
- **Ajayi, Koussoubé & Dao (World Bank PRWP 10239; CGD WP 628), Burkina Faso.**
  - *Design:* Randomized childcare at 18 of 36 public-works sites. **RCT.**
  - *Effects:* Women's employment, financial outcomes and child development improved. Take-up was about 25%.
  - *Gap:* An older-sibling schooling outcome was not found in snippets.
  - *Sources:* [CGD](https://www.cgdev.org/publication/effects-childcare-women-and-children-evidence-randomized-evaluation-burkina-faso); [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wbk:wbrwps:10239).

### Inferences
- **Colombia:** The limited under-3 childcare plausibly binds for young mothers. The Colombian (Medellín) evidence, however, suggests childcare raises *search/participation* more than *employment*, so labor demand for young low-skill women may also bind.
- **Colombia:** The Rio and Mexico results imply that in multigenerational Colombian households, the margin moved by childcare may be **another household member's** labor (grandmother, adolescent sibling) rather than the mother's. This is directly relevant to the older-daughter hypothesis (Q3).

### Gaps
- No verified estimate of HCB or CDI (De Cero a Siempre) effects on maternal employment, and none on **young** mothers specifically.
- The gender split of the Rio older-sibling effect was not visible in snippets.

## Q3. KEY ITEM — Do older daughters substitute for mothers' home production? Effects of maternal employment, childcare or new siblings on older daughters' schooling, domestic work and NEET status

### Takeaway
**Evidence exists, but it is thin, mostly from South Asia and Africa, and mixed in sign.**
- *Strongest causal support:* India's NREGS rollout (Shah & Steinberg, JHR 2021) found that adolescent girls leaving school moved into unpaid domestic work, substituting for their mothers.
- *Supporting:* Older Kenyan evidence that higher maternal wages and childcare costs reduce girls' (not boys') enrollment. The Rio daycare lottery, where adolescent siblings 15+ became more likely to work.
- *Counter-evidence:* NREGS work by **mothers** improved children's schooling, especially girls', via bargaining power (Afridi et al.).
- **I found no causal study from Colombia or Latin America that estimates the effect of maternal employment on older daughters' NEET/NINI status.** This looks like a genuine gap and an opening for the user's original idea.

### Cited Findings
- **Shah & Steinberg (2021), "Workfare and Human Capital Investment: Evidence from India," *Journal of Human Resources* 56(2): 380–405** (NBER w21543, IZA DP 9486).
  - *Design:* Staggered NREGS rollout across districts. **Causal (DiD / rollout).**
  - *Main effects:* Exposure reduces adolescent enrollment by 1–3.5 pp and raises adolescent labor by 4 pp.
  - *By sex:* Boys substitute into market work. **Girls substitute into unpaid domestic work, "in place of their mothers."**
  - *Younger children:* Exposure at ages 2–4 improves outcomes (income effect).
  - *Note:* Magnitudes come from abstracts and summaries, and the working-paper and published numbers may differ.
  - *Sources:* [IDEAS JHR](https://ideas.repec.org/a/uwp/jhriss/v56y2021i2p380-405.html); [NBER Digest](https://www.nber.org/digest/nov15/workfare-and-human-capital-investment-evidence-india); [EconPapers IZA](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:iza:izadps:dp9486).
- **Lokshin, Glinskaya & Garcia (2004), "The Effect of Early Childhood Development Programmes on Women's Labour Force Participation and Older Children's Schooling in Kenya," *Journal of African Economies* 13(2): 240–276** (World Bank WPS 2376, 2000).
  - *Design:* A structural/econometric model of the joint childcare, maternal-work and schooling choice using 1990s data. **Not a quasi-experiment.**
  - *Childcare price:* Higher childcare prices reduce mothers' market work and **reduce girls' schooling, with no effect on boys'**.
  - *Mother's wage:* A higher mother's wage **raises boys' enrollment but lowers girls'**.
  - *Interpretation:* The authors read this as older daughters providing substitute childcare.
  - *Sources:* [EconPapers JAE](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:jafrec:v:13:y:2004:i:2:p:240-276); [World Bank OKR](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/entities/publication/2df810b1-dad1-566f-a4b8-311d00e7a676).
- **Attanasio et al. (2022), Rio de Janeiro daycare lottery** (see Q2). **RCT.**
  - *Siblings:* Free daycare for a 0–3 child raised employment of **co-resident adolescent siblings aged 15+** (and of grandparents).
  - *Interpretation:* Consistent with adolescents having supplied care before the younger child got a slot.
  - *Fade-out and gender:* Effects faded by 2015. The **gender of these siblings was not visible** in snippets, and the schooling outcomes for them were not seen.
  - *Sources:* [NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w30653); [J-PAL PDF](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/sites/default/files/research-paper/WP4285_Public-childcare-in-Brazil_Attanasio-et-all_Nov22.pdf).
- **IPA evidence review, "A Multigenerational Perspective on Childcare" (2024).**
  - *Effects:* One childcare study it covers found older siblings aged 10–14 were 4.9 pp more likely to be enrolled and spent about three more hours on school and homework. **The country/study could not be identified from snippets.**
  - *Spillovers:* Effects on older siblings and grandparents were sometimes as large as on mothers.
  - *Source:* [IPA review PDF](https://poverty-action.org/sites/default/files/2024-06/A%20Multigenerational%20Perspective%20on%20Childcare_Evidence%20Review.pdf).
- **CGD blog, "Doing Well in Life? Thank Your Big Sister."** It summarizes evidence that:
  - Older sisters do much of the childcare in low- and middle-income countries.
  - Community preschools in **Madagascar** improved older siblings' education.
  - A **Turkish** vaccination campaign improved older sisters' literacy.
  - In rural **Pakistan**, older sisters' schooling raised younger brothers' outcomes, tied to the older sister's childcare role.

  The primary papers were not retrieved, so they are **not verified**. Source: [CGD blog](https://www.cgdev.org/blog/doing-well-life-thank-your-big-sister).
- **Dammert (2010), "Siblings, child labor, and schooling in Nicaragua and Guatemala," *Journal of Population Economics*.**
  - *Findings:* Older girls spend more time in domestic work than their younger siblings, and older boys do more market and domestic work. The pattern holds regardless of the younger sibling's sex.
  - *Design:* **Sibling-difference/descriptive, not a quasi-experiment** (from the abstract).
  - *Source:* [EconBiz record](https://econbiz.de/Record/siblings-child-labor-and-schooling-in-nicaragua-and-guatemala-dammert-ana/10003922821).
- **"Leveling the Intra-household Playing Field: Compensation and Specialization in Child Labor Allocation" (2010), *Research in Labor Economics* vol. 31.**
  - *Findings:* Households randomly given Nicaragua's CCT reduced child labor more for older boys and lagging children. That is, they partly compensated for intra-household differences.
  - *Authorship:* Recalled as **Del Carpio & Macours (not verified)**; the search listed it next to Dammert's work.
  - *Source:* [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1108/S0147-9121(2010)0000031012).
- **Afridi, Mukhopadhyay & Sahoo (2016), "Female labor force participation and child education in India: evidence from the NREGS," *IZA Journal of Labor & Development*** (IZA DP 6593; Young Lives WP 95).
  - *Data and design:* Young Lives panel, with district trends controlled. **Quasi-causal.**
  - *Effects:* A larger maternal share of household NREGS workdays **raises children's attendance and grade attainment, particularly girls'**. Fathers' participation has negative effects.
  - *Mechanism:* Mothers' bargaining power, beyond income.
  - *Implication:* **Counter-evidence to a simple substitution story.**
  - *Sources:* [EconPapers IZA DP 6593](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:iza:izadps:dp6593); [UK gov R4D](https://www.gov.uk/research-for-development-outputs/young-lives-working-paper-95-female-labour-force-participation-and-child-education-in-india-the-effect-of-the-national-rural-employment-guarantee-scheme).
- **Islam & Sivasankaran (NREGA; Harvard WP).**
  - *Design:* DiD on the NREGA rollout.
  - *Findings (secondary summary):* Younger children spend more time in school, while **older children spend more time working outside the household**.
  - *Gap:* The domestic-work and gender breakdown was not seen.
  - *Sources:* [Sivasankaran CV](https://scholar.harvard.edu/sites/scholar.harvard.edu/files/asivasankaran/files/cv_anitha_sivasankaran.pdf); [citing review (core.ac.uk)](https://core.ac.uk/download/83954352.pdf).
- **Cornell CFNPP WP 131 (literature review).** It notes evidence that girls' education can suffer when daughters are kept home to care for siblings, and that high childcare costs reduce both maternal work and girls' schooling. [Cornell WP131](http://www.cfnpp.cornell.edu/images/wp131.pdf)
- **World Bank PRWP 10305, "Maternal Work and Children's Development" (review of causal studies across 10 countries).**
  - *Findings:* Maternal work mostly does not harm child development, and the schooling evidence is the thinnest outcome area.
  - *Older children:* The excerpt mentions older children taking on more domestic responsibilities, but whether that finding is causal is unclear from snippets.
  - *Source:* [World Bank PDF](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099413302142327891/pdf/IDU0ddfa53760d59004f670a7a9049595a20ba6d.pdf).
- **Searches that came up empty.** I found no study of older-sibling effects for Mexico's Estancias Infantiles ([Calderón WP](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bdm:wpaper:2014-07) covers mothers and husbands only), Peru's Cuna Más, Burkina Faso's childcare RCT (outcome tables not reached), or Colombia (a Spanish-language search returned only press and think-tank pieces).
- **Colombian descriptive context.**
  - *ENUT:* About 8 in 10 girls aged 10–17 do unpaid domestic or care work (press report: [La República](https://www.larepublica.co/economia/ocho-de-cada-diez-mujeres-en-el-pais-se-dedican-a-trabajo-domestico-no-remunerado-3814737)).
  - *ANIF:* Early allocation of care to girls shapes gender roles ([Bloomberg Línea](https://www.bloomberglinea.com/latinoamerica/colombia/trabajo-domestico-y-de-cuidado-no-remunerado-una-realidad-de-las-ninas-colombianas/)).
  - These are **secondary/press sources, not peer-reviewed**.

### Inferences
- **Colombia:** The user's hypothesis has a credible causal analogue (Shah & Steinberg), a supporting structural result (Lokshin et al.) and supporting lottery evidence (Rio siblings 15+). It also faces credible counter-evidence (Afridi et al.).
  - **Sign condition:** The sign plausibly depends on (i) whether there are **young children (under 5) or dependent elders** at home, (ii) the **income/bargaining effect** of mothers' earnings versus the time-substitution effect, (iii) the daughter's age (15–24 versus younger), and (iv) whether **grandmothers or other adult women** co-reside.
  - **Prediction:** Substitution should be strongest in households with under-3 siblings, no co-resident grandmother, low income, and where formal under-3 care is unavailable. All of these conditions are common in Colombia.
- **Colombia:** The Rio result suggests a testable corollary. **Expanding under-3 childcare (CDI/HCB) should reduce older sisters' NINI status** (by raising their schooling or employment), not only raise mothers' employment.

### Gaps
- **No causal LatAm or Colombian estimate** links maternal employment shocks to older daughters' NEET/NINI status or domestic hours. This is the central gap and appears to be a contribution opportunity.
- The gender breakdown of the Rio adolescent-sibling employment effect, and its schooling effects, are unverified (full text blocked).
- The Madagascar, Turkey, Pakistan and Tanzania sibling papers, and Edmonds (2006) on sibling differences in child labor, are **not verified**.

## Q4. Do conditional cash transfers reduce girls' domestic work and raise schooling (NEET-relevant margins)?

### Takeaway
CCTs (PROGRESA, Familias en Acción) raised enrollment and **reduced children's domestic work**. For girls, the drop in domestic work was smaller than the rise in schooling: many combine school and chores, so chores are not displaced one-for-one. Colombian evidence shows reductions in children's domestic work, but sex-specific results were not verified.

### Cited Findings
- **Skoufias & Parker (2001), "Conditional cash transfers and their impact on child work and schooling: evidence from the PROGRESA program in Mexico," *Economía* (IFPRI FCND DP 123).**
  - *Design:* Randomized village rollout, analyzed with difference-in-differences. **Quasi-experimental/RCT-based.**
  - *Girls' domestic work:* Reduced participation and/or hours at all ages.
  - *Girls' schooling vs work:* The fall in total work was smaller than the rise in schooling, so girls combine domestic work and school.
  - *Sources:* [AgEcon PDF](https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/15950/files/dp01123b.pdf); [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/p/fpr/fcndbr/123.html).
- **Attanasio, Fitzsimons, Gómez, Gutiérrez, Meghir & Mesnard (IFS WP06/01; published 2010 in *Economic Development and Cultural Change* as "Children's Schooling and Work in the Presence of a Conditional Cash Transfer Program in Rural Colombia"; full author list from memory, not verified).**
  - *Program:* Familias en Acción.
  - *Schooling:* Enrollment of 14–17-year-olds rose by 5–7 pp.
  - *Domestic work:* Younger children's participation fell by about 10–13 pp in more urbanized rural areas, with no effect in more rural areas. Income-generating work was unaffected.
  - *Gap:* Sex-specific results were not seen.
  - *Sources:* [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/p/ifs/ifsewp/06-01.html); [IFS](https://ifs.org.uk/journals/childrens-education-and-work-presence-conditional-cash-transfer-program-rural-colombia).

### Inferences
- **Colombia:** CCT conditionality probably keeps girls enrolled up to 17–18 while leaving domestic loads partly intact. Once conditionality ends (after secondary school), the domestic load may become binding and appear as "oficios del hogar" NINI status at 18–24. This is a plausible life-cycle link worth testing with GEIH age profiles by Familias en Acción eligibility.

### Gaps
- No evidence was found on CCT effects on **young adult (18–24) NEET status** of former beneficiaries by sex in Colombia.

## Q5. Do safety, harassment and mobility constraints affect young women's education and work?

### Takeaway
Credible evidence from India and Brazil shows that women pay large costs (in college quality, fares and time) to avoid harassment, and that reducing travel cost or risk (bicycles) substantially raises girls' secondary enrollment.

### Cited Findings
- **Borker (2021), "Safety First: Perceived Risk of Street Harassment and Educational Choices of Women," World Bank PRWP 9731.**
  - *Setting and data:* Delhi University (77 colleges), combining student surveys, route mapping and crowdsourced safety data (SafetiPin, Safecity). **Revealed-preference / structural.**
  - *Women:* Willing to pick a college in the bottom half of quality over a top-quintile college for a 1 SD safer route.
  - *Men:* For the same safety gain, men would only move from a top-20% to a top-25% college.
  - *Costs:* Women spend more on transport and accept longer commutes.
  - *Counterfactual:* Making all routes safe closes about 5% of the college-rank gender gap.
  - *Sources:* [World Bank PDF](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/723631626710146405/pdf/Safety-First-Perceived-Risk-of-Street-Harassment-and-Educational-Choices-of-Women.pdf); [VoxDev](https://voxdev.org/topic/education/perceived-risk-street-harassment-and-college-choice-women-delhi).
- **Muralidharan & Prakash (2017), "Cycling to School: Increasing Secondary School Enrollment for Girls in India," *AEJ: Applied* 9(3): 321–50.**
  - *Program and design:* Bihar's bicycle program, evaluated with a triple difference (boys and Jharkhand as comparisons). **Quasi-experimental.**
  - *Enrollment:* Girls' age-appropriate secondary enrollment +30% and the gender gap −40%.
  - *Exams:* +18% of girls sitting the secondary exam and +12% passing it.
  - *Distance:* Effects are concentrated in villages far from secondary schools.
  - *Sources:* [NBER w19305](https://www.nber.org/papers/w19305); [EconPapers AEJ](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:aea:aejapp:v:9:y:2017:i:3:p:321-50).
- **Kondylis, Legovini, Vyborny, Zwager & Andrade, "Demand for 'Safe Spaces': Avoiding Harassment and Stigma," World Bank PRWP 9269.**
  - *Setting and design:* Rio de Janeiro commuter rail, with the price of the women-reserved car randomized. **RCT.**
  - *Willingness to pay:* About one fifth of riders would forgo 20% of the fare to ride in the reserved car.
  - *Stigma:* Implicit Association Tests show that women riding in the public car are stigmatized.
  - *Authors' framing:* Reserved cars are "second best."
  - *Sources:* [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wbk:wbrwps:9269); [VoxDev](https://voxdev.org/node/299249).

### Inferences
- **Colombia:** Urban Colombian commutes (e.g., Bogotá's TransMilenio) and neighborhood insecurity plausibly raise the cost of schooling and jobs for young women. A safety constraint would show up as greater NINI sensitivity to **commute distance or time to the nearest SENA, university or job center** for women than for men.

### Gaps
- I did not search for or verify **Colombia-specific** causal evidence on transport (e.g., TransMiCable), harassment or violence and young women's education or work. This needs a dedicated search.

## Q6. Gender norms and labor-market entry in Latin America; how unpaid work is measured in NEET statistics

### Takeaway
Female participation growth in LatAm decelerated in the 2000s, concentrated among low-education, rural, married women and mothers. The ILO highlights unpaid care as a driver of young women's NEET status, but the standard NEET indicator does not measure unpaid work. Reason-for-inactivity questions and time-use surveys are needed.

### Cited Findings
- **Gasparini & Marchionni (2017), "Deceleration in female labor force participation in Latin America," *Economía*** (CEDLAS WP 2015).
  - *Data:* Microdata from 15 countries, 1992–2012.
  - *Findings:* The slowdown is sharpest for low-education, rural and married women, mothers, and women with low-earning partners. The suggested driver is boom-era partner earnings and social assistance reducing the need to work.
  - *Design:* Descriptive/decomposition, **not causal**.
  - *Sources:* [LSE repository](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/123208); [IDB blog](https://blogs.iadb.org/igualdad/en/?p=3220).
- **ILOSTAT blog on rural young women.**
  - *Rates:* About 30% of young women worldwide are NEET.
  - *Drivers:* Women disproportionately carry childcare, elder care, cooking, and water and firewood collection, and the effect is larger for rural women.
  - *Source:* [ILOSTAT](https://ilostat.ilo.org/blog/not-in-employment-education-or-training-the-reality-for-many-young-rural-women/).
- **ILO vs national (India PLFS) NEET estimates.** The comparison suggests that how unpaid work and domestic duties are measured and classified varies significantly across frameworks. [VVGNLI comparison](https://www.vvgnli.gov.in/sites/default/files/NEET%20Rates%20ILO%20and%20PLFS%20Comparison.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Colombia:** In GEIH, "oficios del hogar" as main activity is a self-reported inactivity category. It does not distinguish a heavy care load from little activity. Linking NINI status to ENUT hours (or the GEIH unpaid-work module, if available) is needed to separate "care-constrained" from "discouraged/idle" female NINIs.
- **Colombia:** The Gasparini & Marchionni pattern (an income effect from partners' earnings) suggests early **unions** may reduce young women's participation partly through a household income effect, not only through norms.

### Gaps
- I did not find a precise ILO global share of female NEETs citing care as the reason for inactivity.
- I did not find LatAm causal evidence on **gendered entry jobs** for young women; Jensen (India) is the closest analogue.

## Q7. Synthesis — which gender/household mechanisms have strong evidence, which are plausible for Colombia, and how to test them with Colombian data

### Takeaway
1. **Strong evidence:** early marriage and births reduce schooling; childcare raises maternal participation; mobility and safety costs reduce girls' schooling.
2. **Weaker or contested:** a large direct effect of teen motherhood on labor supply (Agüero finds about none in Colombia).
3. **Plausible but untested in LatAm:** the older-daughter substitution mechanism. Its causal support comes from India (Shah & Steinberg) and suggestive Kenya/Brazil results, with Indian counter-evidence. **It appears to be an open research gap for Colombia.**

### Cited Findings
- **Strong (causal, multiple settings):**
  - Early motherhood or marriage lowers schooling: Agüero (Colombia), Field & Ambrus (Bangladesh).
  - Marriage lowers private wage work: Assaad, Krafft & Selwaness (MENA).
  - Childcare raises maternal participation and employment: Martínez & Perticará (Chile), Berlinski & Galiani (Argentina), Buen Comienzo (Medellín, participation only).
  - Grandmother childcare matters: Talamas (Mexico).
  - Mobility and safety: Muralidharan & Prakash; Borker; Kondylis et al.
  - Sources: [Agüero](https://scioteca.caf.com/handle/123456789/1727); [Field & Ambrus](https://dash.harvard.edu/bitstream/1/3200264/2/ambrus_earlymarriage.pdf); [Assaad et al.](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/femeco/v28y2022i2p247-279.html); [J-PAL Chile](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/impact-extending-childcare-female-labor-chile); [Berlinski & Galiani](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/2847/); [Buen Comienzo](https://banrep.gov.co/es/borrador-882); [Talamas](https://publications.iadb.org/publications/english/document/Grandmothers-and-the-Gender-Gap-in-the-Mexican-Labor-Market.pdf); [Muralidharan & Prakash](https://www.nber.org/papers/w19305); [Borker](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/723631626710146405/pdf/Safety-First-Perceived-Risk-of-Street-Harassment-and-Educational-Choices-of-Women.pdf); [Kondylis et al.](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wbk:wbrwps:9269)
- **Weak or selection-driven:** Teen motherhood has little effect on labor supply once selection is handled. Sources: [Agüero](https://ideas.repec.org/p/dbl/dblwop/1727.html); [Hotz, McElroy & Sanders](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:uwp:jhriss:v:40:y:2005:i:2:p683-715)
- **Mixed (older-daughter substitution):** Supported by [Shah & Steinberg](https://ideas.repec.org/a/uwp/jhriss/v56y2021i2p380-405.html), [Lokshin et al.](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:jafrec:v:13:y:2004:i:2:p:240-276) and [Attanasio et al. Rio](https://www.nber.org/papers/w30653); against it, [Afridi et al.](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:iza:izadps:dp6593).

### Inferences — plausibility for Colombia and proposed tests (all inferences)

1. **Older-daughter substitution (the user's original idea).** It is plausible given Colombia's limited under-3 care, the share of female NINIs in "oficios del hogar", high fertility among low-income households, and rising maternal participation. Five tests:
   - **(a) GEIH household roster, local female labor demand.** Link daughters aged 15–24 to their co-resident mother (parentesco + "orden"). Instrument mother's employment with a **shift-share local female labor demand shock**: municipality or department industry mix × national growth in female-intensive sectors. Estimate effects on the daughter's NINI status and main activity = "oficios del hogar".
     - *Triple-difference heterogeneity:* daughters vs sons; households with vs without under-5 children; with vs without a co-resident grandmother.
     - *Prediction:* positive effects concentrated in daughters with young siblings and no grandmother.
   - **(b) CDI / De Cero a Siempre expansion.** Use staggered municipal openings of Centros de Desarrollo Infantil (ICBF administrative rolls) in an event study (heterogeneity-robust staggered DiD). Outcomes: mothers' employment **and** older sisters' NINI status and enrollment. This mirrors the Rio lottery. *Prediction:* CDI reduces older sisters' NINI status where under-3 siblings are present.
   - **(c) ENUT (2012–13, 2016–17, 2020–21).** Within-household time use: daughters' care and domestic hours by mother's employment status and hours, conditioning on household composition. This is descriptive, but it validates the "oficios del hogar" label and the time trade-off.
   - **(d) Shocks to household care demand.** Births of new siblings (twins as a check), or grandmother deaths or exits in a panel. ELCA (the Uniandes Colombian longitudinal survey) is better than the GEIH cross-section for this. Estimate effects on older daughters' schooling and NINI status, à la Talamas.
   - **(e) Placebo:** sons, and daughters in households without care-dependent members.
2. **Motherhood and early unions.** Plausible and partly established for schooling (Agüero). Possible tests:
   - Replicate the menarche IV with **ENDS**, provided ENDS records age at menarche (**check**), for NINI status and union separately from births.
   - Use **Jornada Única** rollout as a quasi-experiment on adolescent fertility and later NINI status, mirroring Kruger & Berthelon.
3. **Childcare for young mothers.** Plausible. Use CDI/HCB expansion × young-mother status in GEIH. Build on Buen Comienzo's finding that participation rises but employment does not, to separate childcare from labor-demand constraints.
4. **Gendered entry jobs.** Plausible (Jensen analogue). Use shift-share female-job demand (BPO and call-center growth in cities) on young women's schooling, union and NINI status.
5. **Safety and mobility.** Plausible in urban Colombia. Use variation in transit access (new lines or cable cars) or local crime and harassment measures × female in GEIH/ENUT. Colombian causal evidence was not searched in this round.
6. **Measurement.** Reclassify GEIH female NINIs by ENUT-imputed care hours to separate a "care-constrained" from an "other" NINI group. Report NINI rates both with and without heavy unpaid caregivers.

### Gaps
- No verified LatAm causal study tests the older-daughter substitution mechanism. The Rio lottery is closest, but its sibling gender split is unverified.
- Whether ENDS records age at menarche, the exact Agüero data source, and the availability of CDI rollout administrative data were not verified.
- Full texts of all papers were not accessible (WebFetch blocked for nber.org and iza.org). Effect sizes come from abstracts and summaries and should be checked.
