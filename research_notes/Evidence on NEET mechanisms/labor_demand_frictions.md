# Labor-Demand and Job-Search Friction Mechanisms Behind Youth NEET Status: International Causal Evidence and Implications for Colombia

*Method note (2026-11-05): Everything here comes from WebSearch result snippets: abstracts, working-paper pages, RePEc/IDEAS listings, J-PAL/IGC/NBER summaries and central-bank pages. I did not open full texts with WebFetch. Effect sizes therefore come from abstracts or secondary summaries and should be checked against the published tables before citing. Items marked "not verified" were recalled from memory and not confirmed by search. Each "What this suggests for Colombia" bullet is an inference, not a finding.*

## Q1. Do minimum wages, especially age-based or regional ones, reduce youth employment or raise NEET status?

### Takeaway
The cleanest causal evidence comes from age discontinuities in European youth minimum-wage schedules. In Denmark the minimum wage jumps about 40% at age 18 and employment falls about 33%, an elasticity near −0.8 (Kreiner, Reck & Skov 2020). In the Netherlands, separations rise just before birthday-linked wage steps (Kabátek 2021). In Colombia the minimum wage is about 85–90% of the median wage. Banco de la República work links higher minimum-wage "bite" to more informality, especially for youth and less-educated workers. I found no Colombian causal estimate of the minimum wage's effect on NEET status.

### Cited Findings
- **Kreiner, Reck & Skov (2020)**, "Do Lower Minimum Wages for Young Workers Raise Their Employment? Evidence from a Danish Discontinuity," *Review of Economics and Statistics* 102(2): 339–354.
  - Design: regression discontinuity at the age-18 jump in Danish youth minimum wages, using monthly payroll records for the whole population. Causal.
  - Wage effect: hourly wages rise about 40% (about DKK 46) at 18.
  - Employment effect: employment falls about 15 percentage points, a 33% fall in the number employed. There is no jump at ages 17 or 19.
  - Elasticity: the employment elasticity is about 0.6–1.1, with a simple implied value near −0.8.
  - Caveat: the authors cannot fully separate quits from firings, and some legal restrictions on under-18 work lapse at 18.
  - Sources: [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:tpr:restat:v:102:y:2020:i:2:p:339-354); [CEBI summary](https://www.econ.ku.dk/cebi/research/do-lower-minimum-wages-for-young-workers-raise-their-employment/); [LSE eprint](https://eprints.lse.ac.uk/100800/); [commentary on quits vs. firings](https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2017/06/minimum-wage-evidence-danish-discontinuity.html)
- **Kabátek (2021)**, "Happy Birthday, You're Fired! Effects of an Age-Dependent Minimum Wage on Youth Employment Flows in the Netherlands," *ILR Review* 74(4): 1008–1035.
  - Setting: the Dutch minimum wage for ages 15–23 rises in steps with calendar age.
  - Findings (Dutch administrative data): youth employment spells are more likely to end shortly before birthdays, about 2,273 terminations a year, concentrated in supermarkets. The initial employment drop is gradually offset by more hiring after the birthday.
  - Causal (birthday discontinuities).
  - Sources: [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:sae:ilrrev:v:74:y:2021:i:4:p:1008-1035); [IZA DP 9528](https://ideas.repec.org/p/iza/izadps/dp9528.html); [IZA newsroom](https://newsroom.iza.org/en/archive/research/happy-birthday-youre-fired-effects-of-the-age-dependent-minimum-wage-in-the-netherlands)
- **Colombian minimum-wage bite.** In OECD countries the minimum wage is close to 50% of the median wage; in Colombia it exceeds 85%. A related Banco de la República essay puts the Colombian figure at about 90%. City-level Kaitz indices (minimum wage over average wage by city and quarter, computed from GEIH) reached 87.3 in Santa Marta in 2016.
  - Sources: [Banco de la República repository](https://repositorio.banrep.gov.co/bitstreams/86fda998-ed96-45f1-8c61-3601bb2169c0/download); [BanRep ESPE, macroeconomic effects of the minimum wage](https://banrep.gov.co/en/publications-research/espe/macroeconomic-effects-of-the-minimum-wage-in-colombia?page=26)
- **Arango, Flórez, Guerrero & Morales-Rojas (2020)**, "Minimum wage effects on labour informality: heterogeneity across demographic groups in Colombia," *Borradores de Economía* No. 1104, Banco de la República.
  - Design: the working population is split into 16 demographic cells, and the minimum-wage ratio varies across cells and over time. This is panel/cell variation, not a clean natural experiment.
  - Findings: informality rises when the minimum wage is high relative to group productivity, "especially among young people and workers with lower education." Results depend on which informality measure is used.
  - Sources: [Borrador 1104 (RePEc)](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bdr:borrec:1104); [BanRep summary](https://banrep.gov.co/en/minimum-wage-effects-informality-across-demographic-groups-colombia); [repository](https://repositorio.banrep.gov.co/handle/20.500.12134/9810)
- **Conflicting magnitudes for Colombia.** A BanRep ESPE piece reports that a 1-percentage-point rise in the minimum-wage ratio (defined against the 70th percentile of income) raises the probability of informal employment by up to 0.7 pp. A search summary of Arango & Morales-Rojas gives 0.21 pp. The figures likely reflect different specifications and groups; I could not reconcile them without the full texts.
  - Sources: [BanRep ESPE informality](https://banrep.gov.co/en/publications-research/espe/new-evidence-labor-firm-informality?page=0); [BanRep repository](https://repositorio.banrep.gov.co/bitstreams/86fda998-ed96-45f1-8c61-3601bb2169c0/download)
- **Arango & Rivera (2020)**, *Borrador* No. 1107, studies the disemployment effects of the minimum wage in formal Colombian manufacturing. Findings were not retrieved. — [BE 1107 PDF](https://repositorio.banrep.gov.co/bitstream/handle/20.500.12134/9825/be_1107.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Mechanism.** The European RD evidence shows that youth employment at the formal margin is highly elastic to the wage floor (elasticity about −0.6 to −1.1). Firms cut jobs quickly, often just before the cost jump.
- **What this suggests for Colombia (inference).**
  - Colombia's single national minimum wage has no youth discount (apprenticeship contracts are partial exceptions; not verified). At 85–90% of the median, the binding margin for low-income, low-credential youth is likely the formal entry job.
  - The likeliest result is informality rather than open unemployment, consistent with Arango et al. A displaced young person may also move to NEET status if the informal option is poor or unsafe. This would fit the income gradient in NEET rates (about 40% vs. 5% by income quintile), because the minimum wage binds harder for youth whose expected productivity is low relative to it.
  - The European evidence covers formal jobs with full enforcement. Colombian elasticities on the formal margin could be similar, but the total effect on NEET status depends on how many workers absorb into informality.
- **Testable implication.** Cities with a higher Kaitz index, and age–education cells with a higher bite, should show larger NEET and informality rates for 18–24-year-olds. Moments when the bite rises, such as the large real minimum-wage increases of 2022–2024 (magnitudes not verified), should hit these groups hardest.

### Gaps
- I did not find a causal estimate of minimum-wage effects specifically on NEET status, as distinct from informality, for Colombia or any Latin American country.
- I did not search the Brazilian youth minimum-wage literature. Engbom & Moser (2022, AER) is about inequality, not youth (not verified).
- **Maloney & Núñez Mendez (2004)**, "Measuring the impact of minimum wages: evidence from Latin America," a Colombia-heavy study, is recalled but not verified.
- I did not verify whether the UK National Minimum Wage youth-rate discontinuity studies (e.g., Dickens, Riley & Wilkinson) find small effects. If they do, the Danish elasticity is at the high end of the literature (not verified).

## Q2. Do payroll-tax cuts or hiring subsidies targeted at youth raise youth employment?

### Takeaway
Results are mixed and depend on context.
- Sweden's large youth payroll-tax cut raised youth employment by 2–3 pp, mainly by reducing separations. There was no pass-through to youth wages, and the employment gains persisted after repeal.
- France's temporary, unanticipated hiring credit for small firms during the 2009 recession created jobs at close to zero net fiscal cost.
- South Africa's youth Employment Tax Incentive showed little short-run effect in household survey data.
- Colombia's Ley 1429 (2010) had weak take-up and mixed, group-specific effects.
- Colombian evidence on payroll taxes in general (Kugler & Kugler) shows substantial formal-employment elasticities.

### Cited Findings
- **Saez, Schoefer & Seim (2019)**, "Payroll Taxes, Firm Behavior, and Rent Sharing: Evidence from a Young Workers' Tax Cut in Sweden," *AER* 109(5): 1717–63.
  - Reform: the employer payroll tax for ages 19–25 was cut in 2007, cut further in 2009, and extended to age 26.
  - Design: age-based difference-in-differences with administrative data. Causal.
  - Findings: no effect on take-home wages of treated youth. Youth employment rose about 2–3 pp, mainly through fewer separations rather than more hiring. Firms with more young workers expanded, with stronger effects for credit-constrained firms, and shared rents with all workers.
  - Sources: [AEA](https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/aer.20171937); [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:aea:aecrev:v:109:y:2019:i:5:p:1717-63)
- **Saez, Schoefer & Seim, "Hysteresis from Employer Subsidies"** (NBER w26391; reported as *J. Public Economics* 2021). Three years after the subsidy was repealed, employment in the formerly subsidized ages remains at the level reached under the reform. — [RePEc NBER w26391](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:nbr:nberwo:26391)
- **Cahuc, Carcillo & Le Barbanchon**, "Do Hiring Credits Work in Recessions? Evidence from France," IZA DP 8330 (2014). Published as "The effectiveness of hiring credits," reportedly in *REStud* 2019; the outlet is not verified in my searches.
  - Program: a temporary credit, December 2008 to December 2009, for hires paid below 1.6 times the minimum wage in firms with fewer than 10 employees.
  - Design: difference-in-differences comparing firms with 6–10 employees to firms with 10–14 employees, using administrative data on all French firms.
  - Findings: strong, rapid employment effects at a net cost per job of about zero. Treated firms grew 0.8 pp faster. Effects were larger where recruiting was easier, and there was no increase in churning.
  - The authors attribute success to the credit being temporary and unanticipated, and to high unemployment combined with a binding wage floor.
  - Sources: [IZA DP 8330](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:iza:izadps:dp8330); [Bocconi summary](https://www.unibocconi.it/en/news/when-tax-credits-help-employment-recovery)
- **Ranchhod & Finn**, "Estimating the short run effects of South Africa's Employment Tax Incentive on youth employment probabilities," *South African Journal of Economics* (2016).
  - Design: difference-in-differences using the Quarterly Labour Force Survey.
  - Findings: no statistically significant effect on youth employment probabilities in the first six months. An update (SALDRU WP 152, 2015) confirms the null.
  - Critique: treatment timing may be mis-specified, because claims were allowed for hires from 2013 Q4.
  - Later work: Ebrahim, Leibbrandt & Ranchhod (2017, UNU-WIDER) use tax administrative data; their results were not retrieved.
  - Sources: [SALDRU WP 134](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:ldr:wpaper:134); [SALDRU WP 152](https://ideas.repec.org/p/ldr/wpaper/152.html); [UNU-WIDER WP 2017/5](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:unu:wpaper:wp-2017-5)
- **Colombia, Ley 1429 (2010, "Ley de Formalización y Generación de Empleo," often called the first-employment law)**.
  - The law gave tax incentives to firms hiring workers under 28.
  - **Ariza & Cedano (2017)**, difference-in-differences: effects varied across sociodemographic groups of youth. More-qualified youth were less likely to be employed in the quarter after the reform and showed more informality months later. — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/rel/journl/y2017v14n1p40-59.html)
  - **Externado labor observatory (Farné)**: fewer than 15% of eligible firms applied in the first six months, and the law's effect on informality was minor in the short run. — [Externado](https://www.uexternado.edu.co/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/articulo14.pdf); [Boletín 13](https://beta.uexternado.edu.co/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/boletin13-nuevo.pdf)
- **Kugler & Kugler (2009)**, "Labor Market Effects of Payroll Taxes in Developing Countries: Evidence from Colombia," *Economic Development and Cultural Change* 57(2): 335–358.
  - Design: variation in payroll taxes over time and across firms in plant-level manufacturing data (details per working paper; not fully verified).
  - Findings: a 10% increase in payroll taxes lowers formal employment by 4–5%. The working-paper version reports that formal wages fall by only 1.4–2.3%, so shifting to workers is partial. Disemployment effects are larger for production workers.
  - Sources: [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:ucp:ecdecc:v:57:y:2009:i:2:p:335-358); [NBER w13855 summary](https://causalclaims.trfetzer.com/paper/w13855.html)

### Inferences
- **What this suggests for Colombia (inference).**
  - When a wage floor binds, as in Colombia, labor costs cannot shift to workers. That makes formal youth employment sensitive to employer-side costs, consistent with Kugler & Kugler.
  - The Swedish and French evidence suggests cost cuts work through retention (fewer separations) and through small or credit-constrained firms. Both margins are relevant in Colombia.
  - Ley 1429's weak take-up mirrors South Africa's ETI. Administrative frictions, credit-style design and informal firms' ineligibility may blunt subsidies aimed at the very youth most at risk of NEET status.
  - The 2012 tax reform (Ley 1607), which cut parafiscal contributions for workers earning under 10 minimum wages, is a further quasi-experiment on employer-side costs. It is studied in Colombian literature (e.g., Fernández & Villar; Morales & Medina), but I did not verify it here.
  - The post-COVID PAEF and the youth hiring incentive (Decree 688 of 2021 / "Sacúdete") are further candidate quasi-experiments (not verified).

### Gaps
- I did not find youth-specific estimates for Argentina's subsidy programs.
- I did not retrieve the Ebrahim et al. administrative-data estimates for South Africa's ETI.
- I did not verify a rigorous PILA-based evaluation of Ley 1429 using its age-28 eligibility cutoff. An RD on age at hire, or a difference-in-differences at age 28, would be a natural design.

## Q3. Does entering the labor market during a recession cause scarring, and is there developing-country evidence?

### Takeaway
High-income-country evidence is strong and causal. Entering during a downturn causes large initial earnings losses that fade over 10–15 years, through cyclical downgrading into worse employers. Losses are larger and more persistent for less-advantaged entrants. Latin American cohort studies (Argentina, Brazil, 15-country harmonized surveys) also find lasting scars, with informality as a key channel. This evidence supports Colombia's countercyclical NEET rate as a mechanism with persistence.

### Cited Findings
- **Oreopoulos, von Wachter & Heisz (2012)**, "The Short- and Long-Term Career Effects of Graduating in a Recession," *AEJ: Applied* 4(1): 1–29.
  - Setting: Canadian college graduates, 1982–1999, matched university–employer–employee data.
  - Design: variation in regional and national unemployment at graduation.
  - Findings: a typical recession causes an initial earnings loss of about 9% of annual earnings, which halves within 5 years and fades by about 10 years. Graduates start at smaller, lower-paying firms and catch up by switching jobs. Shifts in employer size and wages explain 30–40% of the initial loss. Less-advantaged graduates suffer larger, more persistent losses and can be permanently affected.
  - Sources: [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:aea:aejapp:v:4:y:2012:i:1:p:1-29); [NBER Digest](https://www.nber.org/digest/nov06/w12159.html); [author PDF](https://oreopoulos.faculty.economics.utoronto.ca/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/oreopoulos-et-al-the-short-and-long-term-career-effects-of-graduating-in-a-recession-aej-applied-2012.pdf)
- **von Wachter (2020)**, "The Persistent Effects of Initial Labor Market Conditions for Young Adults and Their Sources," *JEP* 34(4): 168–94.
  - Review findings: large initial effects on earnings, labor supply and wages that fade after 10–15 years, through occupation, mobility and employer channels.
  - Wider effects: on fertility, marriage, crime, attitudes and alcohol use, plus higher mortality in middle age.
  - Source: [AEA](https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/jep.34.4.168)
- **Kahn (2010)**, "The long-term labor market consequences of graduating from college in a bad economy," *Labour Economics* (US, NLSY). The paper is cited in Oreopoulos et al. Its specific results are **not verified** here; my recollection is persistent wage losses of several percent per point of unemployment, lasting up to 15 years.
- **Cruces, Ham & Viollaz**, "Scarring effects of youth unemployment and informality: Evidence from Argentina and Brazil" (AAEP 2011 version). Cohorts exposed to higher unemployment and informality in youth fare systematically worse as adults. Channels differ by country. Design: pseudo-panel of cohorts. — [AAEP PDF](https://bd.aaep.org.ar/anales/works/works2011/Cruces.pdf)
- **Berniell, Gasparini & Marchionni** ("unlucky cohorts")
  - Data: harmonized household surveys from 15 Latin American countries. Cohorts are followed from school to work and observed 10 years later.
  - Findings: scarring for unlucky cohorts. Women from unlucky cohorts control a larger share of family income and are more likely to head their household 10 years after entry.
  - Attribution to these authors is per the search summary.
  - Source: [PDF](https://jnmarshan.github.io/docs/unluckyscarring_web.pdf)
- **Moreno & Sousa (2021)**, in World Bank, *Employment in Crisis*: scarring from initial conditions across four Latin American economies. Crises may shift the formal–informal equilibrium, with long-run welfare costs. — [World Bank (issuu)](https://issuu.com/world.bank.publications/docs/9781464816727/s/13452583)
- **Alves & Varvasino**, "Lasting Scars: The Unequal Impacts of Unemployment in Latin America" (CAF working paper).
  - Argument: scarring may be stronger in unequal middle-income economies.
  - Finding: early unemployment exposure reduces later entrepreneurship.
  - Sources: [CAF](https://scioteca.caf.com/bitstream/handle/123456789/1987/Lasting%20Scars_The%20Unequal%20Impacts%20of%20Unemployment%20in%20Latin%20America.pdf?sequence=1); [WP](https://joaquinvarvasino.github.io/assets/docs/lasting-scars-working-paper.pdf)

### Inferences
- **What this suggests for Colombia (inference).**
  - The countercyclical NEET rate likely has a persistent component. Cohorts reaching age 18–20 in 1999–2000, 2009, 2016–17 (oil-price shock) and 2020 should carry durable deficits in formal employment and earnings.
  - The heterogeneity result (worse for the less advantaged) maps onto the income gradient in NEET rates. Low-income youth lack the networks and credentials needed to climb out of initial cyclical downgrading.
  - In Colombia the "downgrade" margin may be informality or inactivity rather than a smaller formal firm.
- **Testable implication.** Using pooled GEIH/ECH waves (2001–2025), regress later outcomes (NEET status, formality, earnings) on the 23-city unemployment rate in the year the cohort turned 18, or the year it left school. Instrument local unemployment with Bartik shocks (national industry growth × baseline city industry shares).

### Gaps
- I found no Colombia-specific causal study of entry conditions and later NEET status or informality. Moreno & Sousa's four countries were not identified in the snippets; Colombia may be one of them (not verified).
- The full-text effect sizes of the Latin American studies were not retrieved.

## Q4. Do job-search frictions such as spatial mismatch and transport costs keep youth out of work? Evidence from Africa and Latin American cities

### Takeaway
Randomized evidence from Addis Ababa shows that transport subsidies raise search intensity and the short-run probability of formal or permanent jobs for youth. The gains fade by four years, unlike skill-signaling gains. Quasi-experimental Latin American transit evidence (Mexico City subway; Bogotá TransMilenio) shows that better access lowers informality and raises wages near stations, especially in peripheral areas. I found no rigorous employment evaluation of Bogotá's TransMiCable.

### Cited Findings
- **Franklin (2018)**, "Location, Search Costs and Youth Unemployment: Experimental Evidence from Transport Subsidies," *Economic Journal* 128: 2353–2379.
  - Design: RCT in Addis Ababa. Subsidies covered transport to the city center two days a week for a limited period.
  - Findings: higher search intensity and more use of formal search methods. Treated youth were more likely to find good, permanent jobs, with a short-term drop in temporary work.
  - Magnitude: roughly +6–7 pp on permanent employment, against a control mean of 19% at four months. Exact figures vary by version; check the published tables.
  - Interpretation: cash constraints make job seekers abandon the search for good jobs too early.
  - Sources: [LSE](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/87938); [SERC DP](https://cep.lse.ac.uk/pubs/download/sercdp0199.pdf)
- **Abebe, Caria, Fafchamps, Falco, Franklin & Quinn (2021)**, "Anonymity or Distance? Job Search and Labour Market Exclusion in a Growing African City," *Review of Economic Studies* 88(3): 1279–1310.
  - Design: RCT with about 4,000 youth in Addis Ababa. Arms: a transport subsidy, and a job-application workshop that included skill certification.
  - Short run: both arms have large positive effects on finding formal jobs. A policy brief reports the transport arm was 25% more likely to be in formal employment. The workshop also raised stable, open-ended-contract employment.
  - Four years later: the workshop significantly raised earnings, job satisfaction and job duration. The transport-subsidy effects had dissipated.
  - Heterogeneity: gains were concentrated among women and the least educated.
  - Sources: [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:restud:v:88:y:2021:i:3:p:1279-1310.); [Oxford page](https://www.economics.ox.ac.uk/node/1203731); [GLM-LIC brief](https://g2lm-lic.iza.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/05/GLMLIC-Policy-Brief_022-1.pdf); [J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/print/pdf/node/2468)
- **Zárate (2022)**, "Spatial Misallocation, Informality, and Transit Improvements: Evidence from Mexico City," World Bank Policy Research WP 9990.
  - Design: openings of subway lines that were planned years ahead, combined with a spatial general-equilibrium model. Quasi-experimental.
  - Findings: informality falls about 7% near new stations (an earlier draft reported −4 pp). Reallocation to formal jobs amplifies welfare gains by 20–25%.
  - Sources: [World Bank OKR](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/bitstreams/e0bd70b8-49aa-5caf-bda2-6f5cfa05a5a6/download); [EconBiz](https://econbiz.de/Record/spatial-misallocation-informality-and-transit-improvements-evidence-from-mexico-city-z%C3%A1rate-rom%C3%A1n/10013254951)
- **Tsivanidis**, "The Aggregate and Distributional Effects of Urban Transit: Evidence from Bogotá's TransMilenio."
  - Design: commuter-market-access approach with census-tract data. Identification uses historical, unbuilt transit plans.
  - Findings: wages grew about 7% more in blocks within 500 m of stations than in blocks more than 1 km away, more so in the periphery. Low-skilled workers gained access to better-paying central jobs.
  - Caveat: employment-specific results were pending in the 2016 slides. The 2022 version is the more complete one.
  - Sources: [2022 paper](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/55bb98e2e4b0ba843f39599e/t/62882b12d140ce5b51ff6e24/1653091092837/TsivanidisTransMilenio_5.2022.pdf); [IGC slides](https://www.theigc.org/sites/default/files/2016/02/TsivanidisSlides.pdf); [IDB blog](https://blogs.iadb.org/ideas-matter/en/?p=6954)
- **TransMiCable (Ciudad Bolívar, Bogotá)**
  - Guzmán et al.: quasi-experimental comparison with San Cristóbal. Accessibility and satisfaction rose at the first follow-up; gains were smaller during COVID. No employment results were found. — [Repositorio](https://repositorioslatinoamericanos.uchile.cl/handle/2250/8728636?show=full)
  - TrUST/SALURBAL (Uniandes–Drexel–Stanford): mean travel time for mandatory trips fell to 90.2 minutes, and satisfaction rose from 4.4/10 to 5.4/10. Employment is measured as a social determinant, but I found no results for it. — [Stanford](https://med.stanford.edu/ourvoice/our-projects/colombia/Urban-Transformations-and-Health-Methods-for-TrUST.html); [Drexel](https://drexel.edu/lac/data-evidence/policy-evaluations/urban-transformations-health-the-case-of-transmicable-in-bogota)
  - Bogotá's planning office (SDP) lists a 2022 mixed-methods evaluation of TransMiCable's area of influence; no results were retrieved. — [SDP](https://sistemas002.sdp.gov.co/print/35890)

### Inferences
- **What this suggests for Colombia (inference).**
  - Peripheral low-income neighborhoods in Bogotá, Medellín and Cali resemble the Addis setting: jobs are concentrated centrally and commutes take about 90 minutes even after TransMiCable.
  - Search costs probably depress search intensity and formal-job finding for low-income youth, and push them toward informal or temporary work, or toward inactivity once discouraged.
  - Transit improvements in Latin America reduce informality at the extensive margin (Mexico City), suggesting that access is a binding constraint on the formal job of entry.
  - Addis shows that transport subsidies alone have short-lived effects. Spatial frictions are real, but may be second-order next to signaling or credential frictions for long-run outcomes.
  - Bogotá's subsidized fare for SISBÉN users (*tarifa diferencial*/*incentivo SISBÉN*; details not verified) and new TransMilenio or cable lines (TransMiCable, Line 1 of the metro) offer quasi-experiments.

### Gaps
- I did not find evidence for Lima (e.g., Metropolitano or Metro Line 1 employment studies) in this search budget.
- I found no causal study of TransMiCable or MetroCable (Medellín) effects on youth employment or NEET status. A recalled MetroCable literature on crime exists, e.g., Cerdá et al. 2012 (not verified).

## Q5. Do information frictions about skills (signaling/certification) and about the returns to education matter?

### Takeaway
Strong RCT evidence shows that making young workers' skills credibly observable to employers raises employment and earnings. The gains come from shareable certification, not just self-knowledge:
- South Africa (Carranza et al. 2022): employment +17% (+5 pp), weekly earnings +34%.
- Uganda (Bassi & Nansamba 2022): better matching and higher earnings for those employed.
- Ethiopia (Abebe et al. 2021): durable earnings gains after four years.
Separately, information on the returns to education raises schooling (Jensen 2010: +0.20–0.35 years), but not for the poorest households.

### Cited Findings
- **Carranza, Garlick, Orkin & Rankin (2022)**, "Job Search and Hiring with Limited Information about Workseekers' Skills," *AER* 112(11): 3547–83.
  - Design: RCT with young South African workseekers through Harambee; assessment results were disclosed in different ways.
  - Shareable reports: employment +17% (+5 pp), weekly earnings +34%, hourly wages +20%.
  - Reports given only to workseekers: similar effects on beliefs and search, but smaller effects on employment and earnings.
  - Reports given only to firms: more callbacks.
  - Sources: [AEA](https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/aer.20200961); [NSF PAR draft](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10477816)
- **Bassi & Nansamba (2022)**, "Screening and Signalling Non-Cognitive Skills: Experimental Evidence from Uganda," *Economic Journal* 132(642): 471–511.
  - Design: RCT with BRAC Uganda. Certificates of soft skills for vocational-training graduates were disclosed in interviews with small firms.
  - Findings: workers raised their labor-market expectations, and high-ability managers revised skill assessments upward. Positive assortative matching increased, and earnings rose for workers who found jobs.
  - The exact earnings effect is not verified.
  - Sources: [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:econjl:v:132:y:2022:i:642:p:471-511.); [CEPR DP16546](https://repec.cepr.org/repec/cpr/ceprdp/DP16546.pdf)
- **Abebe et al. (2021)**: see Q4. The workshop arm, which certified skills, produced durable four-year gains in earnings and job duration, far exceeding its cost, concentrated among disadvantaged groups. — [Oxford](https://www.economics.ox.ac.uk/node/1203731)
- **Jensen (2010)**, "The (Perceived) Returns to Education and the Demand for Schooling," *QJE* 125(2): 515–548.
  - Design: RCT with 2,250 eighth-grade boys in the Dominican Republic; information on measured returns to secondary school was provided in randomly selected schools.
  - Baseline: boys substantially underestimated the returns.
  - Findings: perceived returns rose by RD$366, and completed schooling rose by 0.20–0.35 years over 4 years. There was no significant effect for the poorest households, suggesting cost constraints. Cost-effectiveness was about 0.23 years per US$100.
  - Sources: [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:qjecon:v:125:y:2010:i:2:p:515-548.); [J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/print/pdf/node/1727?lang=en)

### Inferences
- **What this suggests for Colombia (inference).**
  - Low-income Colombian youth often lack credentials or attended low-signal schools.
  - Employers may therefore screen them out of formal entry jobs under uncertainty about their productivity. The high minimum wage amplifies this: when a firm must pay about 90% of the median wage, the cost of a bad hire rises, and so does the value of a credible signal.
  - Certification interventions, such as SENA competency certification, standardized portable skill reports through the Servicio Público de Empleo, or Saber 11/Saber Pro scores made shareable, are a direct test.
  - The Jensen null for the poorest households suggests information alone will not close the education-margin gap for the bottom quintile. Liquidity constraints also bind.
- **Interaction hypothesis (inference).** The minimum wage and signaling are complements. The returns to a credible skill signal should be larger in cities and cells with a higher Kaitz index.

### Gaps
- I found no Colombian RCT on skill certification or signaling for youth job seekers in this search.
- Colombian experiments on information about returns to education, such as the "Ser Pilo Paga"-related information treatments or the Colombian information experiments at MinEducación, were not searched.

## Q6. Do weak referral networks exclude youth from jobs?

### Takeaway
Experimental evidence shows that referral networks transmit information about worker quality, but selectively:
- Referrers pick high-ability contacts only when their pay depends on the referee's performance, and low-ability referrers cannot find good referees (Beaman & Magruder 2012).
- Referrals are often reciprocal and exclude less-connected workers (Witte, Ethiopia).
Colombian evidence is descriptive: informal contacts dominate job finding, but low-income youth's networks consist of similarly disadvantaged peers.

### Cited Findings
- **Beaman & Magruder (2012)**, "Who Gets the Job Referral? Evidence from a Social Networks Experiment," *AER* 102(7): 3574–93.
  - Design: Kolkata lab-in-field experiment in which participants were paid to refer friends. The fixed fee and performance-based pay were randomized. Sample: 562 men and 407 referrals.
  - Findings: the size of the fixed fee had no effect. High-ability referrers referred high-ability people only when incentivized, and low-ability referrers could not recruit high performers.
  - Sources: [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:aea:aecrev:v:102:y:2012:i:7:p:3574-93); [J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/leveraging-social-networks-job-referrals-india); [World Bank blog](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/impactevaluations/sudoku-quilts-and-job-matches-an-experiment-on-networks-and-job-referrals)
- **Witte**, "Why Do Workers Make Job Referrals?" (IZA DP 18258, 2025), also known as "Job referrals and strategic network formation," Addis Ababa.
  - Design: field experiment in a casual data-entry firm set up by the researcher.
  - Findings: workers mainly refer those who previously referred them. This reciprocity causes productivity losses and excludes less-connected individuals. Peripheral workers use referrals strategically to form links that persist for 18 months. Incentivized referrals reduce reciprocity.
  - This is working-paper evidence.
  - Sources: [IZA DP 18258](https://www.iza.org/publications/dp18258/1/citations/bibtex); [AEA RCT registry](https://www.socialscienceregistry.org/trials/2334); [IGC](https://www.theigc.org/collections/job-referrals-and-strategic-network-formation-experimental-evidence-urban)
- **Colombian descriptive evidence**
  - Restrepo Plaza (GEIH 2008 Q2): informal channels, i.e. personal contacts, are more effective and efficient than formal channels. Their advantage grows with education and age, though "modern/meritocratic" channels lead to faster job finding than relying on social networks alone. — [Universidad de Medellín](https://revistas.udem.edu.co/index.php/economico/article/download/404/362)
  - Ocampo Restrepo (Cali): low-income youth's contacts are mainly other disadvantaged youth, which limits access to better vacancies. — [search summary; source among results](https://core.ac.uk/download/427654573.pdf)
  - Neiva probit study: higher socioeconomic strata use informal channels with wider networks. — [Redalyc](https://www.redalyc.org/journal/3578/357874716004/357874716004_2.pdf)
  - Caveat: the Cali and Neiva links were matched to studies by the search summary. I did not confirm which URL hosts which study.

### Inferences
- **What this suggests for Colombia (inference).**
  - If formal hiring runs through referrals, and referral networks are homophilous by income and neighborhood, low-income youth without employed formal-sector contacts face a structural exclusion.
  - This exclusion is worse in recessions, when fewer network members are employed, which is consistent with countercyclicality.
  - The Beaman–Magruder result implies that networks of low-ability or poorly connected members transmit little useful information.
- **Testable implication.** NEET probability should fall with the formal employment rate of a youth's household members, or of their neighborhood or stratum, measured in GEIH or in PILA by municipality and age. A credible design would use Bartik-style shocks to the employment of parents or neighbors, or plant closures affecting household members, as in the displaced-parent literature (not verified for Colombia).

### Gaps
- I found no causal Colombian study of referral networks and youth employment.
- GEIH's job-search-channel questions (e.g., "¿cómo consiguió su empleo actual?") could support descriptive work, but I did not verify current question wording.

## Q7. Do structural change, deindustrialization and credential inflation reduce entry jobs for low-credential youth?

### Takeaway
Causal evidence on deindustrialization and youth NEET status is thin and mostly from OECD countries or descriptive. Evidence on cyclical "upskilling" of employer requirements is stronger: in slack US labor markets, employers raised skill and education requirements in job postings, explaining at least 30% of the 2007–2010 increase. This is consistent with credential inflation pushing low-credential youth down the queue in downturns.

### Cited Findings
- **Modestino, Shoag & Ballance (2020)**, "Upskilling: Do Employers Demand Greater Skill When Workers Are Plentiful?" *REStat* 102(4): 793–805.
  - Data: US online job postings (tens of millions). Robust to firm × job-title fixed effects; uses troop withdrawals as an exogenous labor-supply shock.
  - Finding: rising numbers of job seekers account for at least 30% of the 2007–2010 increase in skill requirements, which the authors call opportunistic upskilling.
  - Sources: [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:tpr:restat:v:102:y:2020:i:4:p:793-805); [Boston Fed WP](https://www.bostonfed.org/-/media/Documents/Workingpapers/PDF/wp1417.pdf)
- **Premature deindustrialization.** Developing countries' manufacturing shares of employment and value added have fallen since the 1980s at lower income levels than in earlier industrializers. This is descriptive. — [NBER w20935 (Rodrik)](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w20935/w20935.pdf)
- **Manufacturing share and youth NEET status, OECD panel (Korean Labor Institute journal)**: a 1-pp drop in manufacturing's employment share raises youth NEET probability by 0.61 pp. This is a correlational panel estimate and a lower-quality source that I could not verify in detail. — [KLI repository](https://repository.kli.re.kr/bitstream/2021.oak/2625/2/%eb%85%b8%eb%8f%99%ec%a0%95%ec%b1%85%ec%97%b0%ea%b5%ac_%ec%a0%9c17%ea%b6%8c_%ec%a0%9c3%ed%98%b8_1.pdf)
- **SADC (Southern Africa) study**: argues premature deindustrialization drives youth unemployment. Preliminary and descriptive. — [GGA](https://gga.org/is-the-southern-african-development-community-sadc-afflicted-by-premature-deindustrialisation/)
- **ILO (O'Higgins chapter)**: reviews NEET status in developing and emerging economies and notes that the NEET concept has been used mostly for industrialized countries. — [ILO](https://www.ilo.org/publications/chapter-3-summary-neet-developing-and-emerging-countries-what-do-we-know)

### Inferences
- **What this suggests for Colombia (inference).**
  - Colombia's manufacturing employment share has declined. Manufacturing historically provided formal entry jobs for youth without tertiary credentials.
  - If formal entry jobs shifted toward services that require bachileres or técnicos, and employers upskill requirements when applicants are plentiful (Modestino et al.), low-income youth lacking credentials are pushed down the hiring queue precisely in downturns. That would contribute to both the income gradient and the countercyclicality.
  - The Venezuelan migration shock of 2015–2022 increased labor supply in some cities. By Modestino et al.'s logic it could have triggered upskilling of postings, a testable spillover onto native low-credential youth (inference; literature on Venezuelan migration's labor effects in Colombia not searched here).
- **Testable implication.** In Colombian vacancy data (Servicio Público de Empleo postings, or scraped postings such as elempleo/Computrabajo), regress posted education requirements within occupation on local slack (city unemployment, or migrant inflows as a supply shock).

### Gaps
- I found no causal study linking Colombian deindustrialization to youth NEET status.
- I did not search Colombian studies of credential inflation (e.g., job-posting analyses by the Ministerio del Trabajo/SPE or Fedesarrollo).

## Q8. Synthesis: which demand and friction mechanisms have strong evidence, which are plausible for Colombia, and how to test them with Colombian data

### Takeaway
1. **Strongest causal evidence (internationally):**
   - Minimum-wage disemployment for youth at the formal margin, from European age discontinuities.
   - Persistent scarring from recession entry.
   - Credible skill signaling raising youth employment and earnings, from three African RCTs.
   - Employer-cost reductions raising youth employment where wages cannot adjust (Sweden; France in a recession; Colombia's payroll-tax evidence).
2. **Moderate evidence:**
   - Transport and spatial search costs: robust short-run RCT effects that fade, plus Latin American transit studies showing lower informality.
   - Referral-network exclusion: experiments showing selective, reciprocal referrals.
3. **Weakest evidence:** deindustrialization and credential inflation as drivers of NEET status. Upskilling in slack markets is causal for the US, but the link to NEET status is untested.
4. **For Colombia:**
   - Most plausible as first-order drivers: the minimum-wage bite (very high Kaitz), recession-entry scarring through informality or inactivity, and signaling frictions.
   - These likely interact: a high wage floor raises the cost of hiring an unproven worker, and recessions raise the number of applicants and employers' requirements.

### Cited Findings
- Danish RD: −33% employment at the age-18 wage jump. — [CEBI](https://www.econ.ku.dk/cebi/research/do-lower-minimum-wages-for-young-workers-raise-their-employment/)
- Colombia's minimum wage is above 85% of the median, against about 50% in the OECD. — [BanRep](https://repositorio.banrep.gov.co/bitstreams/86fda998-ed96-45f1-8c61-3601bb2169c0/download)
- Recession entry: about 9% initial earnings loss, more persistent for the less advantaged. — [NBER Digest](https://www.nber.org/digest/nov06/w12159.html)
- Latin American cohorts entering during high unemployment or informality fare worse as adults. — [Cruces et al.](https://bd.aaep.org.ar/anales/works/works2011/Cruces.pdf)
- Credible skill reports: employment +17%, earnings +34%. — [Carranza et al.](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10477816)
- Transport subsidy: short-run gain in formal jobs that dissipates by four years. — [Abebe et al.](https://www.economics.ox.ac.uk/node/1203731)
- Mexico City subway: informality −7% near stations. — [Zárate](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/bitstreams/e0bd70b8-49aa-5caf-bda2-6f5cfa05a5a6/download)
- Colombian payroll-tax elasticity of formal employment: 10% higher tax, 4–5% less formal employment. — [Kugler & Kugler](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:ucp:ecdecc:v:57:y:2009:i:2:p:335-358)
- Upskilling in slack markets explains at least 30% of the 2007–10 rise in requirements. — [Modestino et al.](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:tpr:restat:v:102:y:2020:i:4:p:793-805)

### Inferences
**Proposed tests with Colombian data (all inferences or research-design suggestions, not findings):**

1. **Minimum-wage bite (GEIH 23 cities, 2007–2025).**
   - Exposure: a city × quarter Kaitz index (minimum wage over median or average wage, BanRep style), or the fraction of 18–24 workers earning near the minimum, interacted with age × education × income-quintile cells.
   - Outcomes: NEET status, informality and formal employment of 18–24-year-olds.
   - Identification: national minimum-wage changes bite differently across cities (a Card-1992-style "fraction affected" design). The large real increases of 2023 (about 16% nominal; magnitude not verified) give a sharp shock.
   - Prediction: a larger rise in NEET status and informality for low-income youth in high-bite cities. Check for bunching at the minimum in PILA (ingreso base de cotización).
2. **Recession at entry (GEIH/ECH pooled cohorts).**
   - Design: define the cohort by city and year of turning 18, or of leaving school. Regress outcomes at ages 22–30 on the entry-year city unemployment rate, instrumented with a Bartik shift-share (national sector growth × 2005 Census or early-GEIH city sector shares).
   - Outcomes: NEET status, formality and earnings.
   - Prediction: persistent effects larger in the bottom income quintiles.
   - Administrative alternative: PILA's first appearance for entry cohorts gives first-job firm size and wage (cyclical downgrading), following Oreopoulos et al.
3. **Payroll-cost incentives (PILA).**
   - Design: regression discontinuity or difference-in-differences at the age-28 eligibility cutoff of Ley 1429 (2010–2014), and the Ley 1607 (2012) parafiscal cut.
   - Outcomes: formal hiring and separations of youth just under versus over 28. Sweden suggests looking at separations.
4. **Spatial mismatch (Bogotá, Medellín, Cali).**
   - Design: event studies around TransMilenio phase openings, TransMiCable (2018), Medellín MetroCable lines, and the Bogotá Metro Line 1 construction.
   - Outcomes: NEET status and formality of youth by neighborhood or UPZ. GEIH city-level geography is coarse, so the Encuesta de Movilidad, Encuesta Multipropósito Bogotá, or PILA by residence may be needed (not verified).
   - Alternatively, use commuter-market-access measures following Tsivanidis.
   - A natural RCT replication: transport subsidies via TuLlave/SISBÉN fares.
5. **Networks.**
   - In GEIH, relate youth NEET status to the formal employment of other household members.
   - For causal identification, use shocks to parents' formal employment, such as plant closures or mass layoffs visible in PILA, if family links can be made (not verified as feasible).
6. **Signaling.**
   - Test whether returns to credible signals (Saber 11 percentiles, SENA certification) on formal employment are larger in high-Kaitz cities or during slack periods. This is the complementarity hypothesis.
   - Run an RCT providing standardized skill reports through the Servicio Público de Empleo, replicating Carranza et al.
7. **Credential inflation.**
   - Scrape or obtain Servicio Público de Empleo vacancy data. Regress posted education and experience requirements on local unemployment and Venezuelan migrant inflows, following Modestino et al.

**Overall ranking for Colombia (inference):**
1. Minimum-wage bite interacting with signaling uncertainty.
2. Recession-entry scarring through informality.
3. Network exclusion.
4. Spatial mismatch: plausible in large cities, but RCT evidence suggests short-lived effects.
5. Credential inflation and deindustrialization: plausible but least tested.

### Gaps
- No Colombian causal evidence was found on any mechanism specifically for NEET status, as opposed to informality or employment.
- Not covered within this search budget: Brazilian minimum-wage youth studies, Argentine youth subsidies, Lima transit studies, the South African ETI administrative-data results, and Kahn (2010) details. Treat these as not verified.
- Effect sizes come from abstracts and search snippets (WebFetch was not used). Verify against the published tables before citing.
