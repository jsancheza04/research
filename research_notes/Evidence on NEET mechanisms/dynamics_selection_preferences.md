## Q1. NEET flows and transition matrices: how much of the NEET stock is short spells vs. long-term, and how do exit rates differ by gender?

### Takeaway
Every panel study I found shows that the NEET stock contains a large share of short, recurrent spells together with a smaller core of long-term NEETs. In Latin America, many exits from NEET lead into informal jobs. The long-term core is disproportionately female (inactive, often caring for children), poor and less educated. **I did not obtain any actual transition-matrix numbers for Mexico, Brazil, Argentina or Peru, because the full texts were blocked.**

### Cited Findings
**Mexico (ENOE / LFS rotating panel)**
- Ranzani & Rosati (2013), *The NEET Trap: A Dynamic Analysis for Mexico* (UCW WP), use Mexican labour-force-survey panels. They build annual transition matrices for all youth and by gender, poverty status and education, and estimate a "propensity matrix" — [Scribd copy, table of contents](https://www.scribd.com/document/425221690/NEET-TRAP-MEXICO); [CEIS Tor Vergata PDF](https://www-2020.ceistorvergata.it/public/images/UCW/PaperUCW/Research_paper/17._The_Neet_trap_(Mexico).pdf).
- Main conclusion (abstract): "the NEET phenomenon involves an increasing number of youth and … specific groups suffer from a higher risk of being trapped in NEET status, which appears to be very consistent over time." The design is descriptive, not causal — [UK gov R4D](https://www.gov.uk/dfid-research-outputs/the-neet-trap-a-dynamic-analysis-for-mexico).

**Argentina, Brazil and Mexico (multi-country LFS panels)**
- Cunningham & Bustos Salvagno (2011), *Youth Employment Transitions in Latin America*, World Bank Policy Research WP 5521. They use panel labour-force data for Argentina, Brazil and Mexico, decompose transition matrices into propensity-to-move and separation rates, and estimate durations.
- Their typical life cycle: a short spell in informal work, then longer formal spells, then self-employment. Formal workers are less likely to return to school. The design is descriptive — [World Bank OKR](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/handle/10986/3294); [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wbk:wbrwps:5521).

**Brazil (PNAD Contínua panel)**
- Freire & Saboia (2019), *Revista Econômica* (UFF), estimate permanence and transition probabilities with PNADC 2012–2016. They describe it as the first such analysis and find "dynamism" within the NEET group, i.e., substantial flows in and out — [Revista Econômica PDF](https://periodicos.uff.br/revistaeconomica/article/download/35035/24645).
- Freire da Mota's UFRJ doctoral thesis (2018), covering 2004–2015 with logit models, concludes that the NEET population is "dynamic and constantly renewing itself" — [UFRJ thesis PDF](https://www.ie.ufrj.br/images/IE/PPGE/teses/2018/Denise%20Guichard%20Freire%20da%20Mota.pdf).
- **Contrast:** a UFMG study for 2002–2015 reports that time spent as NEET increased, especially at ages 18–29, with "significant temporal dependence" — [UFMG repository](https://repositorio.ufmg.br/handle/1843/55277). The Brazilian evidence therefore shows both churning and stickiness, depending on period and method.
- Kovrova & Lyon (2012/13, UCW) run a cohort (not panel) analysis of NEET dynamics in Brazil and Indonesia using PNAD — [UK gov R4D](https://www.gov.uk/research-for-development-outputs/neet-youth-dynamics-in-indonesia-and-brazil-a-cohort-analysis).

**Argentina (EPH rotating panel)**
- Martínez (UNLP thesis) studies NINIs aged 15–24 over 2003–2018, using the EPH rotation scheme (4 observations over 1.5 years) and a cohort approach. Of NINIs who find a job within 12 months, about **60% are informal** — [UNLP thesis PDF](https://www.me.econo.unlp.edu.ar/wp-content/uploads/Tesis-Martinez-Lorena.pdf).
- Studies combining EPH transition matrices with qualitative work describe youth insertion as cycling between employment, unemployment and inactivity — [Redalyc](https://www.redalyc.org/pdf/3238/323830085003.pdf).

**Peru**
- A mixed-methods study argues that NEET is a poor vulnerability measure where informal labour dominates, and proposes an "urban vulnerable" category that includes precarious workers. It is tested with cross-section and panel data; the panel source (ENAHO?) is **not verified** — [search snippet](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/epub/10.1080/13676261.2019.1587154).
- I found **no** Peru ENAHO-panel NEET transition matrix.

**Europe (EU-SILC)**
- Italy: Contini, Filandri & Pacelli (2019), *Journal of Youth Studies*, "Persistency in the NEET state: a longitudinal analysis," use the EU-SILC 2008–2011 monthly activity calendar. **More than 40%** of Italian youth were NEET for at least 13 of 48 months, and **more than 10%** for (almost) the whole period.
  - Most long-term NEETs were *unemployed*, not inactive. The exception was older women with children, who mostly reported themselves as inactive — [UniTo preprint](https://iris.unito.it/bitstream/2318/1690470/1/JYS%202019%20preprint.pdf); [CEDEFOP copy](https://www.cedefop.europa.eu/files/persistency_in_the_neet_state_a_longitudinal_analysis_1.pdf).
- Southern Europe, EU-SILC 2004–07 vs. 2013–16, Wooldridge dynamic model: long-term persistence is smaller than short-term persistence, and short-term persistence rose after the Great Recession, **especially for male NEETs** — [CEDEFOP PDF](https://www.cedefop.europa.eu/files/being_neet_before_and_after_the_great_recession.pdf).
- UK (LFS five-quarter proxy, 2023–24): among disabled NEET youth, 67% are long-term NEET vs. 34% short-term. The LFS measure does not see status between the observed quarters — [Youth Futures Foundation](https://youthfuturesfoundation.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/YF-NEETS-TRENDS-ANALYSIS-OCT-DEC-2025.pdf).

**Colombia (context)**
- BanRep Borrador, *Jóvenes que ni estudian ni trabajan: dinámica y determinantes en Colombia* (likely the Lasso-Valderrama & Vargas paper; authorship **not verified** from the snippet). It covers 1984–2024 with a cohort approach and studies persistence into adulthood and the community and macro conditions that affect it.
  - A snippet reports that the NINI rate "rises sharply between ages 16 and 18" and that the condition is "highly persistent into adulthood" — [BanRep (ES)](https://banrep.gov.co/es/publicaciones-investigaciones/borradores-economia/jovenes-estudian-trabajan-dinamica); [BanRep (EN)](https://banrep.gov.co/en/publications-research/working-papers-economics/youth-school-work-dynamic).

### Inferences
- **Inference (Colombia):** synthetic-cohort persistence of the NINI *share* is fully compatible with high individual turnover. Evidence from Brazil (PNADC "constantly renewing") and Argentina (60% of exits go to informal jobs) supports hypothesis (a), churning, as a first-order candidate. The Italian calendar data show that a long-term core (≥10%) can coexist with mostly short spells.
- **Inference (Colombia):** the gender split likely differs by mechanism. Female NINIs are more likely to be long-term, inactive and caregiving, so their "persistence" is less about churning. Male NINIs are more likely unemployed or churning through informal jobs. The South-European finding that short-term persistence rose most for men after a shock suggests male NINI rates are more cyclical.
- **Inference:** the GEIH has (to my knowledge, **not verified** here) no official rotating-panel component like ENOE, EPH or PNADC. Flows would have to come from the ELCA, PILA (formal jobs only), or retrospective GEIH questions (time unemployed, previous job).

### Gaps
- No actual transition probabilities (NEET→NEET, NEET→work, NEET→school by gender) were retrieved for Mexico, Brazil, Argentina or Peru; full PDFs were blocked. These are the numbers most needed. Get them from Ranzani & Rosati (2013), Freire & Saboia (2019) and Cunningham & Bustos Salvagno (2011).
- No EU-LFS / Eurofound EU-wide long-term NEET share was found. A Eurofound (2012) report, *NEETs – Young people not in employment, education or training: characteristics, costs and policy responses*, is recalled but **not verified**.
- No Peru ENAHO-panel or Colombian individual-panel NEET transition study was located.

## Q2. State dependence and scarring in NEET status and youth unemployment (dynamic panel and IV designs)

### Takeaway
High-income countries have credible evidence of scarring: past unemployment or NEET status raises future unemployment or NEET probability beyond what individual heterogeneity explains. Designs include Gregg's IV on UK cohort data, Mroz & Savage's semiparametric NLSY approach, and dynamic random-effects probits with Wooldridge initial conditions on EU-SILC. Measured genuine state dependence is usually **modest relative to raw persistence**. I found almost no credible developing-country estimates.

### Cited Findings
- **Gregg (2001)**, "The Impact of Youth Unemployment on Adult Unemployment in the NCDS," *Economic Journal* 111(475): F626–F653. UK 1958 birth cohort.
  - The abstract argues that preventing a build-up of youth unemployment (and low achievement) may reduce the concentration of adult unemployment among a minority of men — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:ecj:econjl:v:111:y:2001:i:475:p:f626-53); [Bath portal](https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/the-impact-of-youth-unemployment-on-adult-unemployment-in-the-ncd/).
  - The identification uses local labour-market conditions at age 16 as an instrument for youth unemployment. This is reported by a related CMPO paper using the same instrument — [Bristol CMPO WP97](https://www.bristol.ac.uk/media-library/sites/cmpo/migrated/documents/wp97.pdf). The exact IV specification and effect sizes are **not verified** from the primary text.
- **Mroz & Savage (2006)**, "The Long-Term Effects of Youth Unemployment," *Journal of Human Resources* 41(2): 259–293, NLSY79. Semiparametric methods address the endogeneity of past behaviour.
  - They find persistence in unemployment and a rational "catch-up" human-capital response.
  - The 2003 WP abstract says unemployment up to ten years earlier still lowers earnings — [JHR](https://jhr.uwpress.org/content/41/2/259); [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:uwp:jhriss:v:41:y:2006:i:2:p259-293).
- **Mussida & Sciulli (2023)**, "Being poor and being NEET in Europe: are these two sides of the same coin?", *Journal of Economic Inequality* 21: 463–482. EU-SILC 2016–2019, 21 countries.
  - Dynamic bivariate probit with correlated random effects, endogenous initial conditions and feedback effects.
  - Finds **genuine state dependence in NEET status** and a NEET↔poverty "vicious circle," strongest when NEET includes the unemployed. Unemployment is the main channel.
  - For NEETs living outside the family of origin, NEET status does not raise poverty, conditional on youth support — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s10888-022-09561-7.pdf); [UniCh manuscript](https://ricerca.unich.it/retrieve/6c177621-ef97-4b45-a575-b2dc99c7c280/Manuscript%20Mussida%20Sciulli%20JOEI%202023.pdf).
- **Southern Europe, Wooldridge dynamic model:** long-term persistence < short-term persistence, and short-term persistence rose after the Great Recession, especially for men — [CEDEFOP](https://www.cedefop.europa.eu/files/being_neet_before_and_after_the_great_recession.pdf).
- **Finland (register data):** being NEET in the previous year is associated with **almost a 3-percentage-point** higher probability of being NEET this year after controlling for heterogeneity. This is small next to raw persistence — [Turku UTUPub thesis "NEET persistence: analysis of heterogeneity in the state dependence of the NEET situation"](https://www.utupub.fi/handle/11111/51011).
- **Developing countries:**
  - Brazil (UFMG, 2002–2015) reports "significant temporal dependence" in NEET status, but the design (true vs. spurious state dependence) is **not verified** — [UFMG](https://repositorio.ufmg.br/handle/1843/55277).
  - Mexico (Ranzani & Rosati 2013) reports consistent "trap" risks for specific groups, descriptive — [UK gov R4D](https://www.gov.uk/dfid-research-outputs/the-neet-trap-a-dynamic-analysis-for-mexico).
  - South Africa (2025, *Development Policy Review*, peri-urban KwaZulu-Natal adolescents, 4 rounds): hopelessness about the future predicts NEET vulnerability. It is a cohort study, not a state-dependence estimate — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:devpol:v:43:y:2025:i:2:n:e12829).

### Inferences
- **Inference (Colombia):** international evidence supports *some* true state dependence (hypothesis b). The careful estimates (Finland ~3 pp; EU long-term < short-term persistence) imply that most observed persistence is heterogeneity or composition, not scarring. A Colombian estimate should separate the two with Wooldridge-type initial-conditions models or instruments such as local labour demand at age of school exit.
- **Inference (Colombia):** where informality is high, scarring may run through *job quality* (informal entry) rather than through *non-employment*. If so, NEET-to-NEET state dependence could be weak while informality is persistent.

### Gaps
- No credible dynamic-panel or IV estimate of NEET state dependence for a Latin American country was found. The UFMG Brazil result has an unverified design.
- Gregg (2001) and Mroz & Savage (2006) effect sizes were not retrieved, only abstracts.
- Gregg & Tominey (2005, *Labour Economics*) on the wage scar of youth unemployment is recalled but **not verified**.

## Q3. Selection and composition: does expanding schooling make non-enrollees more negatively selected?

### Takeaway
There is strong, well-established evidence in the *returns-to-education* literature that expansion draws in lower-ability marginal students and shifts the average quality of each schooling group. By the same logic, those left out of school become more negatively selected. I found **no study that directly applies this to NEET or youth-idleness trends**. That looks like a genuine gap the Colombian paper could fill.

### Cited Findings
- **Carneiro & Lee (2011)**, "Trends in Quality-Adjusted Skill Premia in the United States, 1960–2000," *American Economic Review* (2011; volume listed as 110(6) on EconPapers, likely a typo for 101(6) — **verify**).
  - As college enrollment expands, marginal entrants of lower quality reduce the average quality of graduates. This lowers the college premium by **8 pp** (2009 CEMMAP WP version) or **6 pp** (2010 IZA version). Which figure the published version uses is unconfirmed — [IZA DP 5295](https://repec.iza.org/dp5295.pdf); [CEMMAP](https://cemmap.ac.uk/?p=8898); [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:cpr:ceprdp:8108).
- **Hendricks & Schoellman (2009)**, US 1950–2000: with positive sorting on ability, educational expansion lowers average ability at the margin. Ability changes matter less for the relative wages of high-school and college dropouts — [search snippet, core.ac.uk](https://core.ac.uk/download/213905833.pdf). The exact title and outlet are **not verified**.
- **Huang, Xu & Zhu (2019)**, UK higher-education expansion: marginal entrants came from weaker prior-attainment groups, and expansion significantly reduced returns to education — [Dundee PDF](https://discovery.dundee.ac.uk/ws/files/30284262/Huang_Xu_Zhu_2019_EM.pdf).
- **Brand & Xie (2010)**, *American Sociological Review*: "negative selection hypothesis." Those least likely to attend college benefit most from it. This is about heterogeneous returns, not dropout composition — [Yu Xie Princeton page](https://yuxie.scholar.princeton.edu/document/41).
- **Latin American descriptive evidence consistent with selection:**
  - De Hoyos, Rogers & Székely (2016): about 60% of LAC ninis are in the bottom 40% of the income distribution, and 66% are women.
  - As young women's schooling and employment improved after 1992, the number of male ninis rose 46% — [World Bank OKR](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/entities/publication/83e4ac1b-aa2f-5eb2-ad54-db1acdbb5200/full); [World Bank blog](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/education/nini-youth-latin-america-out-school-and-misunderstood).
- **Colombia, causal evidence on schooling expansion at the margin:** Familias en Acción (urban) reduced high-school dropout by **5.8 pp** and raised male college enrollment by **1.7 pp** (Attanasio et al.). This shows policy pulled marginal youth into school, which mechanically changes who remains out — [VoxDev](https://www.voxdev.org/topic/public-economics/long-term-effects-conditional-cash-transfer-programmes-evidence-colombia).

### Inferences
- **Inference (Colombia):** the Lasso-Valderrama & Vargas finding — rising "only studies" offset by falling "only works," leaving the NINI cohort effect flat — is exactly what a composition model predicts. If expansion pulls into school the youth who would otherwise have worked (the more employable non-students), the remaining non-students are more negatively selected. Their employment rate falls even with unchanged labour demand, holding the NINI share flat. This is hypothesis (c), and it is consistent with the Carneiro & Lee logic applied to the bottom of the distribution.
- **Inference:** a testable implication: across cohorts or municipalities, larger increases in enrollment should predict *lower* employment among non-enrolled youth, *conditional on* local labour demand, and *more disadvantaged observables* (parental education, stratum, test scores) among non-enrollees.

### Gaps
- No paper was found that directly estimates the composition effect of schooling expansion on NEET or youth-idleness rates. Searched terms included "negative selection dropouts," "composition youth idleness" and "educational expansion dropouts outcomes."
- Card & Lemieux (2001) on dropout and enrollment trends is recalled but **not verified**.

## Q4. Reservation wages: remittances, cash transfers, social pensions and youth or household labour supply

### Takeaway
Evidence is mixed and setting-specific. Cross-sectional and matching studies in Mexico, Central America and Colombia associate remittance receipt with lower labour-force participation, including higher youth idleness among young men in Guatemala. The cleanest quasi-experimental evidence (the South African pension) is *contested*:
- cross-section designs find prime-age men work less;
- panel designs find household members work *more* through migration.

Transfers can also relax credit and childcare constraints rather than raise reservation wages.

### Cited Findings
**South Africa old-age pension**
- **Bertrand, Mullainathan & Miller (2003)**, "Public Policy and Extended Families: Evidence from Pensions in South Africa," *World Bank Economic Review* 17(1): 27–50 (NBER WP 7594, 2000).
  - Design: cross-section discontinuity at eligibility ages (women 60, men 65); the pension is about 2× African per-capita income.
  - Finds sharp drops in prime-age men's labour supply in pensioner households, larger when the recipient is a woman.
  - The effect shrinks with household size and the man's education. The **oldest son** reduces work most. Women's labour supply is unaffected — [NBER w7594](https://www.nber.org/papers/w7594); [EconPapers WBER](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:wbecrv:v:17:y:2003:i:1:p:27-50).
- **Ardington, Case & Hosegood (2009)**, "Labor Supply Responses to Large Social Transfers: Longitudinal Evidence from South Africa," *AEJ: Applied* 1(1): 22–48.
  - Design: Africa Centre panel in KwaZulu-Natal, with individual and household fixed effects through pension entry and exit.
  - Prime-age employment **rises**, mainly via **labour migration**. Mechanisms are financing the migrant's job search and grandparents' childcare.
  - They attribute the opposite BMM result to cross-sectional selection of who co-resides with pensioners — [NBER w13442](https://www.nber.org/papers/w13442); [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:aea:aejapp:v:1:y:2009:i:1:p:22-48).

**Brazil rural pensions (1991 reform)**
- **de Carvalho Filho (2012)**, "Household Income as a Determinant of Child Labor and School Enrollment in Brazil: Evidence from a Social Security Reform," *Economic Development and Cultural Change* 60(2): 399–435 (IMF WP 08/241). Children aged 10–14.
  - Benefits raised girls' school enrollment and reduced their work, closing the enrollment gap by **~20%** for girls living with a beneficiary. Only benefits received by women reduced girls' work. The abstract reports no effect for boys, though one summary disagrees.
  - Related work: the reform reduced elderly rural labour supply, and husbands *increased* labour supply in response to wives' benefits — [EconPapers EDCC](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:ucp:ecdecc:doi:10.1086/662576); [IMF WP](https://imf.org/en/Publications/WP/Issues/2016/12/31/Household-Income-As-A-Determinant-of-Child-Labor-and-School-Enrollment-in-Brazil-Evidence-22388); [MIT DSpace](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/41802).
- **No evidence on youth aged 15–24 or prime-age adults was found** for this reform.

**Remittances**
- **Amuedo-Dorantes & Pozo (2012)**, "Remittance Income Volatility and Labor Supply in Mexico," *Southern Economic Journal* 79(2): 257–276. ENIGH 2000–2008.
  - Male and female labour supply falls with remittance income, and labour supply buffers remittance volatility. This is not youth-specific.
  - An earlier 2006 paper reports that men shift toward informal jobs and that women's labour supply falls only in rural areas — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/wly/soecon/v79y2012i2p257-276.html); [Dallas Fed WP 1007](https://www.dallasfed.org/~/media/documents/research/papers/2010/wp1007.pdf).
- **Sousa & García-Suaza (2018)**, "Remittances and Labor Supply in the Northern Triangle," World Bank Policy Research WP 8597. Propensity score matching on 2006 and 2014 surveys for El Salvador, Guatemala and Honduras.
  - Remittances are associated with lower labour-force participation, especially for women: **−13 pp for Salvadoran women**.
  - They are also associated with a **lower likelihood that young adults are in school or at work**, robust to selection bias for **young men in Guatemala**.
  - Some evidence of support for self-employment; no robust effect in Honduras 2014 — [World Bank OKR](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/handle/10986/30446); [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wbk:wbrwps:8597); [press coverage, El Mundo SV](https://diario.elmundo.sv/economia/las-remesas-disminuyen-la-oferta-laboral-en-el-salvador-afirma-banco-mundial).
- **Colombia:** a study of the Coffee Axis (Universidad de Antioquia, *Lecturas de Economía*/BanRep version, c. 2010) finds that remittances significantly reduce participation in poor households but not richer ones. Of AMCO households, 18.3% receive remittances, and ~85% of remittance value funds recurrent spending — [Redalyc](https://www.redalyc.org/pdf/861/86120154005.pdf); [UdeA](https://revistas.udea.edu.co/index.php/coyuntura/article/view/7665).
  - Remittances reached 1.7% of GDP in 2016–17, equal to 2.3% of household disposable income and 2.7% of consumption (BanRep researchers, via Portafolio) — [Portafolio](https://www.portafolio.co/economia/las-remesas-equivalen-al-2-7-del-consumo-527055).

**Long-run CCT effects (not NEET-specific)**
- **Parker & Vogl** (Progresa, Mexico): childhood exposure raised schooling and migration to urban areas, and raised women's probability of working by **11 pp (41%)** — [VoxDev](https://www.voxdev.org/topic/social-protection/long-term-effects-cash-transfers-mexicos-progresa); [NBER w24303](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w24303/w24303.pdf).
- **Colombia, Familias en Acción:** dropout −5.8 pp, male college +1.7 pp. The Medellín research line reports long-run effects on crime, informality and financial inclusion — [VoxDev](https://www.voxdev.org/topic/public-economics/long-term-effects-conditional-cash-transfer-programmes-evidence-colombia).

### Inferences
- **Inference (Colombia):** hypothesis (d), higher reservation wages from remittances or transfers, has plausible but mostly *non-causal* support in LAC. The best LAC youth-specific evidence (Sousa & García-Suaza, Guatemala young men) is matching-based.
- **Inference (Colombia):** the South African debate warns that **co-residence with parents or pensioners is endogenous**. Youth who stay home are selected, so cross-sectional "living with parents → more NINI" correlations should not be read as a reservation-wage effect.
- **Inference (Colombia):** Ardington et al. suggest transfers can *finance job search and migration* and so raise employment. The net sign for Colombia is ambiguous ex ante.
- **Inference:** given the size of remittances in Colombia (1.7% of GDP and concentrated in the Coffee Axis and Valle), municipal shift-share remittance exposure is a promising instrument. Examples: origin-region migrant stocks × US/Spain labour-market shocks, or exchange-rate shocks.

### Gaps
- No causal study found on remittances or transfers → *youth NEET* specifically in Colombia.
- No study found on Colombia's Colombia Mayor (social pension) or Ingreso Solidario and co-resident youth labour supply.
- The Acosta (El Salvador) and Calero, Bedi & Sparrow (Ecuador) remittance–schooling papers are recalled but **not verified** in this search.

## Q5. Preferences, leisure technology and mental health

### Takeaway
- **Leisure technology:** the main causal-ish US evidence (Aguiar, Bils, Charles & Hurst 2021) attributes a meaningful share of young men's hours decline to gaming. It is identified from a structural demand system, not an experiment, and it is contested by cross-country comparisons. In developing countries, internet and mobile access is mostly found to **raise** employment.
- **Mental health:** NEET status is strongly associated with prior and concurrent mental-health problems in UK and Mexican cohorts, but causality runs both ways.

### Cited Findings
**Leisure technology**
- **Aguiar, Bils, Charles & Hurst (2021)**, "Leisure Luxuries and the Labor Supply of Young Men," *Journal of Political Economy* 129(2): 337–382 (NBER WP 23552, 2017). US men aged 21–30, ATUS since 2004.
  - Gaming and recreational computing are "leisure luxuries."
  - Innovations since 2004 explain about **half the increase in young men's leisure**. They predict a market-hours decline of **1.5–3.0%**, equal to **38–79% of the differential decline** relative to older men.
  - The design is structural (a leisure demand system), not experimental — [NBER w23552](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23552); [EconPapers JPE](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:ucp:jpolec:doi:10.1086/711916); [UChicago PDF](https://knowledge.uchicago.edu/record/10413/files/Leisure-Luxuries-and-the-Labor-Supply-of-Young-Men.pdf).
- **Kopytov, Roussanov & Taschereau-Dumouchel (2023)**, "Cheap Thrills: the Price of Leisure and the Global Decline in Work Hours," *Journal of Political Economy Macroeconomics* 1(1): 80–118 (NBER WP 27744). A macro model and cross-country evidence show that falling recreation prices push hours down — [NBER w27744](https://nber.org/papers/w27744); [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:ucp:jpemac:doi:10.1086/723717).
- **Critiques:**
  - Kimbrough (2018), using ATUS, finds gaming time largely substitutes for other screen leisure and attributes falling employment more to labour demand — [IPUMS bibliography](https://bibliography.ipums.org/citations/show/f4823dee-9cbe-3f6c-88eb-eea49766b609).
  - A Manhattan Institute commentary (policy, non-peer-reviewed) finds no similar young-men employment divergence in Japan, the UK or South Korea — [Manhattan Institute](https://manhattan.institute/article/more-gaming-doesnt-mean-less-work).
- **Developing countries (internet as a job-search and labour-demand technology):**
  - Chiplunkar & Goldberg (NBER WP 30741): 3G rollout across developing countries raises employment for men and women and female participation, with shifts to service wage jobs — [causalclaims summary](https://causalclaims.trfetzer.com/paper/w30741.html); [Yale EGC summary](https://egc.yale.edu/sites/default/files/2024-03/GGG_3G%20paper_summary%20final.pdf).
  - Hjort & Poulsen: staggered submarine-cable arrival in Africa raised employment (outlet *AER* 2019, **not verified**) — [World Bank Jobs blog](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/jobs/jobs-and-mobile-phones-africa).
  - Vietnam (Trinh, IARIW WP, 2SLS): mobile internet lowers informal employment — [IARIW](https://iariw.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/Trinh_Hanoi.pdf).
  - **No study found** on smartphones or gaming as a *leisure* technology reducing youth labour supply in a developing country.

**Mental health**
- **Goldman-Mellor et al. (2016)**, "Committed to work but vulnerable: self-perceptions and mental health in NEET 18-year olds from a contemporary British cohort," *Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry* 57(2): 196–203. E-Risk cohort, N=2,232, born 1994–95.
  - 11.6% were NEET at 18. **~60%** of NEETs had ≥1 mental-health problem in childhood or adolescence vs **~35%** of non-NEETs.
  - Concurrent mental-health and substance problems were associated with NEET status independently of prior vulnerability.
  - NEETs reported *greater* work commitment and job-search diligence, so this is "not a motivation issue." The design is a longitudinal association, not causal — [eScholarship PDF](https://escholarship.org/content/qt5c478560/qt5c478560.pdf?t=p6yq5x); [Duke faculty DB](https://fds.duke.edu/db/aas/pn/faculty/terrie.moffitt/publications/253111).
- **Mexico City, 8-year cohort** (*European Child & Adolescent Psychiatry*, 2017), "NEET adolescents grown up," N=1,071, ages 12–17 at baseline.
  - NEETs differed least from youth who only worked. The largest difference was **increased incident suicidal behaviour** among NEETs — [BVS record](https://busqueda.bvsalud.org/portal/resource/pt/mdl-28528425); [Altmetric](https://www.altmetric.com/details/20336863).
- **Blanchflower & Bryson (NBER WP 33111, 2024):** in Latin America, including Mexico, the age peak of depression and anxiety has moved from the late 40s/early 50s to the **mid-20s** — [NBER PDF](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w33111/w33111.pdf).
- **Prevalence:** adolescent depression and/or anxiety in Latin America is estimated at 17% (Colombia) to 26% (Argentina) — [Priebe et al., BMJ Open 2021 protocol](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/10135145/1/e052339.full.pdf).
- **South Africa:** hopelessness about the future predicts NEET vulnerability — [DPR 2025](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:devpol:v:43:y:2025:i:2:n:e12829).

### Inferences
- **Inference (Colombia):** hypothesis (e), leisure technology, has weak external validity for Colombia. US evidence is structural and contested, and developing-country evidence mostly finds internet *raises* employment. A gaming-driven fall in "only works" would need to show up as larger declines among young men in higher-connectivity, higher-income households. The NINI population is concentrated among poor women, so the profile does not fit well.
- **Inference (Colombia):** mental health is a plausible *amplifier* of persistence (both cause and consequence). The age shift in LAC depression peaks (Blanchflower & Bryson) makes it timely. Goldman-Mellor's "committed but vulnerable" result argues against a pure preference or motivation story.

### Gaps
- No Colombian or LAC study links smartphone, gaming or internet *leisure* use to youth labour supply or NEET.
- No Chile (ELPI) or Brazil (Pelotas) NEET–mental-health cohort result was found.
- No causal (IV / natural experiment) estimate of mental health → NEET was found.

## Q6. Synthesis: strength of evidence, plausibility for Colombia, and tests with Colombian data

### Takeaway
The best-supported mechanisms internationally are:
1. **Churning plus a heterogeneous long-term core.** Stock persistence is much larger than individual state dependence (EU-SILC, PNADC, EPH).
2. **Composition and selection under educational expansion.** Strong in returns-to-schooling work, but untested for NEET — a likely contribution.

Modest **true state dependence** is supported in Europe and the US. **Reservation-wage effects** of remittances and transfers are plausible but contested. **Leisure technology** is the weakest for Colombia. **Mental health** is a strongly correlated, plausibly bidirectional factor.

### Inferences
**Plausibility ranking for Colombia (inference):**
1. **(c) composition** — fits the flat cohort NINI effect with rising "only studies" and falling "only works" almost by construction. It is the most important to test, and the international gap makes it publishable.
2. **(a) churning** — very plausible given informality (60% informal exits in Argentina; churning in Brazil).
3. **(b) state dependence** — likely present but modest.
4. **(d) reservation wages** — plausible regionally (Coffee Axis, Valle) and for men; sign ambiguous.
5. **(e) leisure tech** — least plausible as a main driver.
6. **Mental health** — plausible amplifier; data-limited.

**Proposed tests with Colombian data (inference):**
- **Churning vs. persistence (a):**
  - The **ELCA** panel (2010, 2013, 2016, 2019 waves) gives individual NINI histories: the share of NINIs at t who are NINI at t+3, and the distribution of the number of waves spent as NINI.
  - **PILA** linked to GEIH or ELCA gives formal-job spells. Note PILA misses informal jobs, so it bounds churning from below.
  - **GEIH** retrospective items (duration of job search, months since last job, previous job) give pseudo-flows and spell durations by gender.
  - Compare stock-based synthetic-cohort persistence with individual persistence.
- **State dependence (b):**
  - Dynamic random-effects probit with Wooldridge initial conditions on ELCA.
  - IV using municipal labour demand at age 16–18 (Bartik shift-share from industry composition × national sector growth), as in Gregg (2001).
  - Compare the lagged-NINI coefficient with raw persistence.
- **Composition (c):**
  1. **Reweighting / DiNardo-Fortin-Lemieux:** hold the cohort composition of non-enrollees on parental education, stratum and household income, and recompute employment of non-students across cohorts.
  2. **Bounds:** treat cohort enrollment gains as drawn from the top of the non-student employability distribution (Lee-type bounds).
  3. **Exploit enrollment shocks:** Familias en Acción urban expansion (2007), Ser Pilo Paga (2014–18) and Generación E as shifters of enrollment by municipality and cohort. Test whether non-enrolled youth's employment falls where enrollment rose, conditional on local labour demand.
  4. **Saber 11 test scores:** check whether average scores of those who do not continue to tertiary education decline across cohorts.
- **Reservation wages (d):**
  - GEIH remittance-receipt questions (household income from abroad).
  - BanRep municipal or departmental remittance inflows × shift-share exposure (migrant-origin stocks × US/Spain unemployment or exchange-rate shocks).
  - Event studies around Colombia Mayor eligibility (age thresholds of co-resident elders) — the BMM/ACH design, with a panel (ELCA) to avoid co-residence selection.
  - Ingreso Solidario rollout (2020) as a transfer shock.
- **Leisure tech and mental health (e):**
  - Heterogeneity by household internet access (GEIH ICT module) and municipal 4G rollout timing (MinTIC data), for young men vs women.
  - Test whether "only works" fell more where connectivity rose, conditional on labour demand. Note that the international evidence predicts the *opposite* sign via job search.
  - Mental health: Encuesta Nacional de Salud Mental 2015 (cross-section) and any ELCA well-being items.
