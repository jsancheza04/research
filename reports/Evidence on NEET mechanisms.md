# Graduation gaps, care and churn shape Colombia's NINIs

## 0. Bottom line

The international and Latin American evidence points to four or five stories behind Colombia's NINI patterns. **First [Likely]:** the 16–18 jump is mainly a post-graduation transition problem. An 11-grade system releases graduates at 16–17 into rationed SENA and public-university places and into a formal entry market where the minimum wage is above 85% of the median, so employers screen unproven youth hard. **Second [Likely]:** the female majority reflects a different population, not the same women who dominate tertiary enrolment. These are poor women with early unions and births who move into long-term care-related inactivity. Abroad, early motherhood causally lowers schooling, but the only Colombian IV study finds little direct effect on labour supply once selection is handled. **Third [Likely]:** the persistent cohort share hides two groups. One is a stable female care core; the other is a mostly male segment churning between informal jobs, unemployment and NINI spells, and that segment is the countercyclical part. **Fourth [Speculative but well-motivated]:** the flat cohort effect comes from negative selection. Expanded schooling pulled the most employable non-students into school, which leaves the non-enrolled pool weaker and lowers "only works" without changing NINI. **Fifth [Speculative]:** two further channels may be layered on top: an age-18 gate (work permits for 15–17-year-olds, cédula-based contracting, and the libreta militar for men), and older daughters substituting for working mothers in home production. No study anywhere tests the age gate, and older-daughter substitution has causal support only from India. **The most important gap:** there is no individual-level evidence on how Colombian youth move into and out of NINI status after graduating, by sex and by age at graduation. Without it, a temporary waiting room cannot be told apart from a permanent care core or from informal churning, and every mechanism above stays a hypothesis.

## 1. Scope and limits of this evidence base

The four underlying note files were built from web-search snippets, abstracts, repository pages (RePEc/IDEAS, NBER, J-PAL, World Bank OKR, BanRep) and secondary summaries. **No full text was opened**, because WebFetch was blocked in those sessions. Every effect size below is as reported in an abstract or summary and must be checked against the paper's tables before it is cited. Items the note-takers recalled but could not confirm are marked **"not verified"**, and some versions disagree (for example, working-paper and published magnitudes); I flag these where they arise. Statements under "What this suggests for Colombia" are inferences carrying the labels **[Established] / [Likely] / [Speculative]**, not findings.

Two citation corrections apply. "Baron, Popova & Sánchez (2016), *Following Mexican Youth: A Short-Run Study of Time Use Decisions*, World Bank Policy Research WP 7534" **does exist** and is cited in Borrador 1363. The notes' failure to find it came from a wrong title in the original brief, and none of the notes retrieved its content, so its findings are not summarised here. Explanation labels follow the PoC (Section 6): E1 liquidity, E2 perceived returns, E3 youth labour demand and legal-age barriers, E4 care, motherhood and partnership, E5 school quality and dropout, E6 training supply, E7 discouragement or preferences, E8 measurement. Three mechanisms recur in the evidence but are **not in the PoC's E-list**: churning, true state dependence and composition (negative selection). I label them D1–D3 and suggest adding them, because F4 and F5 cannot be addressed without them.

## 2. What the evidence says, fact by fact

### F1. The 5%→22% jump between 16 and 18 coincides with graduation, not with a wage step

**Candidate mechanisms.** Seven candidates are on the table:
- **E3a, age gate.** Work at 15–17 needs a permit; the cédula and full contracting capacity arrive at 18.
- **E3b, military status.** The libreta militar and draft uncertainty affect men only.
- **E1/E6, admission rationing.** Graduates who miss SENA or public-university cutoffs face a waiting period.
- **E3c, entry-cohort crowding.**
- **E4, end of CCT conditionality.** Conditionality keeps girls enrolled to about 17–18; after that, the domestic load binds.
- **E3d, age-based wage step.** Probably absent in Colombia (see Section 4).
- **E8, measurement.** Rolling-quarter seasonality around the end of the school year, and conscripts living outside private households (both flagged in the PoC).

**What this suggests for Colombia (inference).** The jump most plausibly marks a **post-graduation transition gap** [Likely]. Its timing matches the end of grade 11, and the Cartagena profile shows that many NINIs are secondary *graduates* rather than dropouts, which points away from E5 as the main driver of the jump. The international literature establishes that employers react sharply to **age-specific costs at the exact birthday** [Established abroad]. In Colombia, however, the relevant age rules sit on the hiring-permission side (permit at 15–17, cédula at 18), not the wage side. By analogy, an age gate should show up as a jump in **formal entries** at 18, the mirror image of Denmark's separations. That is [Speculative]: no study anywhere measures what happens to graduates who finish school *before* reaching full legal labour-market age.

The Brazilian minimum-age RD implies that a ban on hiring under-18s binds mainly in **formal, inspected** jobs, so 16–17-year-olds who want work may take informal jobs instead [Likely]. A waiting room should therefore show up in formal employment but not in total employment. Rationed admission plausibly adds a second waiting margin [Likely]. Gap-year evidence suggests that waiting is costly through lost experience, but no paper reports what Colombian or Chilean *rejected* applicants actually do. The military channel is male-specific and [Speculative]. The only Colombian estimate, a master's thesis, is null. The PoC's institutional table also lists Law 1780/2016 art. 20 and Law 1861/2017 art. 42, while the notes could not confirm the current legal status of the libreta requirement after 2022–2024. This needs checking.

One discipline check matters here. A pure waiting room predicts that NINI peaks at 17–18 and falls by 19–20. F4 says the share stays high, so the age gate can explain at most the *timing* of entry, not the stock.

**Cleanest test with Colombian data.** Exploit the school-entry cutoff (age at transición), which creates within-cohort variation in graduation age, using SIMAT linked to Saber 11, SPADIES/SNIES and PILA. **Conditional on graduating**, children born just after the cutoff graduate older. If an age gate binds, they should have *shorter* post-graduation spells without formal enrolment or work. Main threats are redshirting and retention, so check density and first-stage strength.

Complement this with an RD at the 18th birthday in PILA. The prediction is a jump in formal **entries** and no jump in separations, which rules out a Danish-style wage step. Use still-enrolled youth as a placebo. For men, run a difference-in-discontinuities at 18 by sex, before and after the libreta reforms. For rationing, run admission-cutoff RDs (UNAL, Univalle, SENA entry exam) on NINI status 6–18 months later, split by whether the applicant is under or over 18.

### F2. Two-thirds of NINIs are women in "household chores", while women dominate tertiary enrolment

**Candidate mechanisms.**
- **Composition (D3/E4).** Two different female populations: tertiary students and poor, early-partnered women.
- **E4a.** Early births and unions.
- **E4b.** Scarce childcare for young mothers.
- **E4c.** Older daughters substituting for working mothers in home production.
- **E4d/E1.** Income effects from partners and transfers.
- **E3e.** Weak demand for low-skill female labour ("gendered entry jobs").
- **E3f.** Safety and mobility costs.
- **E8.** "Oficios del hogar" is a self-reported label that does not separate heavy care loads from idleness.

**What this suggests for Colombia (inference).** The "gender paradox" is most plausibly **composition**: two distinct female populations [Likely]. Female NINIs are drawn from poor households with early unions and births. Colombian correlational studies already associate female NINI with early partnership, children under five and domestic work. Agüero's IV result implies that early motherhood's main cost in Colombia runs through **lost schooling and pre-existing disadvantage**, not a large direct cut in labour supply [Likely]. Policy that only delays births may therefore move NINI less than raw gaps suggest.

The MENA evidence suggests that **unions**, common in Colombia as cohabitation, are a separate margin from births and bite specifically on *formal private wage work* [Speculative for Colombia]. The Medellín result, where childcare raised participation but barely raised employment, implies that childcare scarcity is real but sits alongside weak demand for young low-skill women [Likely].

Older-daughter substitution is **plausible but untested in Latin America** [Speculative]. India's NREGS rollout shows girls replacing mothers' domestic work, the Kenyan model and the Rio lottery point the same way, and Afridi et al. show the opposite sign through maternal bargaining power. The sign should depend on whether there are under-3 siblings, whether a grandmother co-resides, and household income. Finally, "household chores" as a GEIH main activity does not measure hours, so part of the female excess may be a classification artefact [Likely]. The ILO–PLFS comparison shows NEET rates are sensitive to how domestic duties are classified ([VVGNLI](https://www.vvgnli.gov.in/sites/default/files/NEET%20Rates%20ILO%20and%20PLFS%20Comparison.pdf)).

**Cleanest test with Colombian data.** Start with a descriptive split that costs nothing. Divide female care NINIs (PoC group N3) into those **with an own child under five** and those **without own children but with young siblings at home**. The second group is the direct footprint of older-daughter substitution. Validate hours with ENUT.

Causally, there are two designs. (i) A staggered event study of **CDI / De Cero a Siempre** openings by municipality, on both mothers' employment *and* older sisters' NINI status. The prediction is that effects concentrate where under-3 siblings are present, mirroring Rio. (ii) In the GEIH roster, link daughters aged 15–24 to their mothers and instrument the mother's employment with a shift-share shock to local female-intensive labour demand. Use a triple difference across daughters vs sons, households with vs without under-5 children, and with vs without a co-resident grandmother. For motherhood itself, run event studies around first birth and first union in ELCA. If ENDS records age at menarche (not verified), replicate Agüero with NINI and union as separate outcomes.

### F3. NINI reaches ~40% in the bottom income quintile and ~5% in the top

**Candidate mechanisms.**
- **E3g.** A minimum wage that binds hardest on low-productivity youth, with informality or inactivity as the fallback.
- **E3h.** Signalling frictions: low-signal schools and no credentials.
- **E3i.** Homophilous referral networks.
- **E3j.** Spatial mismatch.
- **E1.** Liquidity constraints on tertiary entry and job search.
- **E2.** Low perceived returns.
- **E4.** Early fertility concentrated among the poor.
- **E7/E8 (reservation wages).** Remittances or transfers raising reservation wages.
- **E8 (reverse causality).** A NINI youth earns nothing, so households with NINIs are mechanically poorer.

**What this suggests for Colombia (inference).** The gradient is almost certainly overdetermined [Established as a descriptive fact; the mechanisms are Likely]. Several international findings converge on one point: the formal **entry job** is the binding margin for poor youth. The minimum wage sits at 85–90% of the median, wage shifting is partial, and employers face uncertainty about unproven workers, so the formal entry job is costly to offer and hard to obtain without a credible signal [Likely]. Colombian evidence shows that the adjustment runs mainly through **informality** rather than open unemployment. NINI is the residual for those whose informal option is poor, or for whom care already binds [Speculative on the size of the NINI margin].

The signalling RCTs imply that minimum-wage bite and signalling are **complements**: the higher the mandated wage, the greater the value of a credible certificate [Speculative, untested anywhere]. Homophilous networks and long peripheral commutes plausibly add to exclusion [Speculative]. Jóvenes en Acción shows that a certifiable credential acquired *after* 18 raises formal employment for exactly this population [Established for training effects; untested for NINI as an outcome].

Two cautions apply. First, the quintile gradient is partly mechanical, because household income omits the NINI's own earnings [Likely bias, size unknown]. Second, co-residence with parents is endogenous, as the South African pension debate shows (Bertrand, Mullainathan & Miller vs Ardington, Case & Hosegood; [NBER w7594](https://www.nber.org/papers/w7594), [NBER w13442](https://www.nber.org/papers/w13442)). Cross-sectional "household income → NINI" correlations therefore overstate any reservation-wage story.

**Cleanest test with Colombian data.** Run a Card-style "fraction affected" design on GEIH 2007–2025: city × quarter Kaitz index interacted with age × education × quintile cells, with outcomes NINI, informality and formal employment for ages 18–24. The large nominal increase around 2023 (≈16%; not verified) provides a sharp shock. Add a complementarity test: are the formal-employment returns to Saber 11 percentile or SENA certification larger in high-Kaitz cities? Measure the income gradient using **per-capita income excluding the youth**, or SISBEN score at 16, to remove mechanical reverse causality.

In Latin America, exits run mostly into informal jobs. Colombia likely has a **mostly male churning segment** (unemployed or between informal jobs) and a **mostly female long-term care core** [Likely]. Italy's long-term inactive group of older women with children matches the second.

True state dependence is probably present but **modest** relative to raw persistence [Likely, by analogy with Finland's ≈3 pp]. In a high-informality economy, scarring may show up mainly as *informal* employment rather than repeated non-employment [Speculative]. Mental health is a plausible amplifier of persistence. LAC evidence shows the age peak of depression and anxiety moving to the mid-20s (Blanchflower & Bryson, [NBER w33111](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w33111/w33111.pdf)), and the UK "committed but vulnerable" finding argues against a pure motivation story [Speculative for Colombia].

**Cleanest test with Colombian data.** Use the **ELCA** panel (wave years to be verified) to compute individual NINI→NINI rates and the distribution of waves spent NINI, by sex and typology. Compare them with the synthetic-cohort persistence. Bound churning from below with **PILA** formal spells for Saber 11 cohorts; this is a lower bound because informal work is invisible. Use GEIH retrospective items (search duration, months since last job) for pseudo-flows. For state dependence, estimate a dynamic random-effects probit with Wooldridge initial conditions on ELCA, plus a Gregg-style IV using municipal Bartik labour demand at ages 16–18.

**What this suggests for Colombia (inference).** "Only studies" up, "only works" down, NINI flat: this is what a composition model predicts [Speculative, but the most parsimonious account]. Policies such as Familias en Acción demonstrably pulled marginal youth into school. If those drawn in were disproportionately the youth who would otherwise have worked, the remaining non-students are more negatively selected, and their employment rate falls with no change in labour demand. The returns-to-schooling literature establishes this selection logic at the top of the distribution. **No study applies it to NEET or idleness trends**, which makes this a clean contribution.

Two alternatives need to be ruled out. Demand-side erosion (a shrinking manufacturing share, upskilled requirements) would also lower "only works" [Speculative; evidence is US-causal for upskilling, correlational for deindustrialisation]. And a flat total may hide **opposite sex trends**, as in the LAC report where male NINI growth offset female gains [Speculative for Colombia; check directly].

**Cleanest test with Colombian data.** (i) DiNardo–Fortin–Lemieux reweighting: hold the cohort composition of non-enrollees fixed on parental education, stratum and income, and recompute non-student employment across cohorts. If composition drives the pattern, the decline in "only works" largely disappears. (ii) Lee-type bounds that treat enrolment gains as drawn from the top of the non-student employability distribution. (iii) Enrolment shifters by municipality and cohort (Familias en Acción urban expansion, Ser Pilo Paga, Generación E). Predict lower employment among *non-enrolled* youth where enrolment rose, conditional on Bartik local demand. (iv) Track the Saber 11 scores of graduates who do not continue: they should decline across cohorts. Run every decomposition separately by sex.

## 3. What is most plausibly happening

The stories in Section 2 can be stitched into one life-course account. It is disciplined by the evidence but contains untested links, which I flag as I go.

**Stage 1: a graduation-age waiting room at 16–18.** Grade 11 ends at about 16–17, so each year a cohort of graduates, many still minors, reaches the end of compulsory schooling at once. Three walls meet them.
- Public tertiary and SENA places are rationed by exam.
- Formal hiring of under-18s requires a permit and carries restrictions, and the cédula arrives only at 18. Men also carry unresolved military status.
- The entry wage is set at more than 85% of the median, which makes any unproven hire expensive.

Top-quintile families bridge the gap with private tertiary education. Bottom-quintile graduates wait, or take informal work that a ban on hiring minors does not reach (the Brazilian enforcement result). This explains the *timing* of F1 and part of F3 [Likely as a transition gap; Speculative for the specific age-18 legal gate]. The weakest link is the age gate itself. There is no direct evidence anywhere on graduates who finish before legal adulthood, and the only Colombian libreta estimate is a null master's thesis.

**Stage 2: the waiting room empties in two directions, split by sex.** For young men, the spell ends in **churning** between informal jobs, unemployment and NINI. Argentine panels show about 60% of NINI exits go into informal jobs, and Brazil's NEET pool "constantly renews itself". This segment is the **countercyclical** one, and cohorts entering at the 1999 and 2020 peaks plausibly carry durable informality scars, worse for the poor (F4, F6) [Likely for churning, by analogy; Speculative for Colombian scarring magnitudes]. Weakness: no Colombian flow data have been analysed.

For young women from poor households, the spell converts into **early union, early birth and care work**, and the NINI state becomes long-lived "household chores". Agüero's Colombian IV suggests that motherhood itself barely lowers labour supply. What matters is who becomes an early mother and the schooling she loses [Likely]. Weakness: Agüero's coefficients and data source were not seen.

A second, untested female channel is the **older daughter**. As mothers' participation rises (and Colombian women's participation has been rising), daughters aged 15–24 in households with infants and no grandmother may absorb home production, as Indian girls did under NREGS (Shah & Steinberg) [Speculative]. This could add to the female care core even among women with no child of their own. It is the most novel piece and also the most fragile. There is no Latin American test, the Rio sibling effect has no visible sex split, and Indian evidence on maternal bargaining power runs the other way. The Rio lottery's implication, that cheaper infant care frees the *other* household members first, gives a clean Colombian prediction for CDI expansion.

**Stage 3: a flat cohort effect produced by sorting, not stagnation.** Across cohorts, schooling expansion (free schooling, CCTs, tertiary financing) pulled the most employable would-be workers into "only studies". Those left out are more negatively selected, so "only works" falls while NINI stays flat (F5) [Speculative but parsimonious]. The weak link is that no NEET study anywhere has tested this, and the evidence base is the returns-to-schooling literature at the top of the distribution. If the flat total instead hides falling female and rising male NINI, as in the LAC report, part of the story is male deterioration in entry demand, not composition.

**How it adds up internationally (F7).** Several layers stack on top of one another: young graduation, a binding floor at an unusually high bite, a weak apprenticeship bridge, concentrated care burdens and definitional choices. Together they plausibly push Colombia above peers [Speculative]. The stock persistence that Lasso-Valderrama & Vargas document is therefore best read as **two populations with different dynamics**, a churning male searching group and a stable female care group, not a single trapped mass.

**What is strongest and what is weakest.** The strongest pieces are the descriptive facts themselves and the causal findings abroad: early births and unions lower schooling, childcare raises maternal participation, recession entry scars the disadvantaged most, youth employment is highly elastic to birthday-specific costs, and credible signals raise youth employment. The weakest are the Colombia-specific links: the age-18 legal gate, older-daughter substitution, the size of true state dependence, and composition as the explanation of F5. **E1 (liquidity)**, the central bank paper's preferred reading, has little direct support in these notes. Jensen's null for the poorest households is consistent with cost constraints, and Jóvenes en Acción shows that subsidised training works for the poorest quintile. But no evidence links liquidity specifically to the 18-year timing or the female composition, so E1 is better seen as one input to Stage 1 than as the master explanation [Speculative].

## 4. Mechanisms with weak fit for Colombia

**Leisure technology (E7).** Aguiar, Bils, Charles & Hurst (2021, *JPE* 129(2)) attribute a 1.5–3.0% market-hours decline among young US men, 38–79% of their differential decline, to gaming and recreational computing ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23552)). The identification is structural and contested; Kimbrough (2018) attributes falling employment more to demand ([IPUMS](https://bibliography.ipums.org/citations/show/f4823dee-9cbe-3f6c-88eb-eea49766b609)). In developing countries, connectivity *raises* employment: 3G rollout (Chiplunkar & Goldberg, [summary](https://causalclaims.trfetzer.com/paper/w30741.html)) and African submarine cables (Hjort & Poulsen; outlet not verified). Colombia's NINIs are mostly poor women in care, the opposite of the profile this mechanism needs.

**An age-differentiated youth minimum wage (Danish/Dutch mechanism).** Colombia's minimum wage is, as far as the notes recall, **not age-differentiated (not verified)**. Apprentices are the partial exception, with rules not verified in the notes. The birthday wage-step mechanism behind Denmark's −33% employment at 18 should therefore be absent. A PILA test showing no spike in separations at 18 would confirm this and isolate the entry-side gate.

**Transport subsidies as a durable remedy.** Spatial frictions are real: in Addis Ababa, Franklin (2018, *EJ*) finds +6–7 pp permanent employment against a 19% control mean at four months ([LSE](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/87938)). But the transport-subsidy effects in Abebe et al. had **dissipated by four years**, while certification effects persisted. TransMiCable studies show better access but report no employment results ([Drexel](https://drexel.edu/lac/data-evidence/policy-evaluations/urban-transformations-health-the-case-of-transmicable-in-bogota)). Spatial mismatch is at most second-order for persistence.

**A large direct labour-supply penalty of teen motherhood.** Credible IV designs (Agüero in Colombia; Hotz, McElroy & Sanders in the US) find small or short-lived labour effects. The channel works through schooling and selection, not through motherhood removing women from work.

**Mandated participation to 18 as a fix.** England's RPA produced modest participation gains and conflicting NEET results, confounded by vocational-course cuts. Mandates without programme supply do not remove idleness.

**The libreta militar as a main driver.** It remains plausible for men, but the only Colombian estimate is null, and the international conscription literature finds schooling-timing effects with small labour effects. It cannot explain a female-majority NINI population.

**Reservation wages from co-residence, remittances or transfers.** The best Latin American youth-specific evidence is matching-based (Guatemalan young men), and the cleanest quasi-experiment (the South African pension) is contested, with panel designs finding that transfers *finance* job search and migration. This is plausible regionally (the Coffee Axis), but its sign is ambiguous.

**Deindustrialisation.** Evidence linking it causally to NEET is correlational or descriptive (KLI panel; Rodrik's premature-deindustrialisation facts, [NBER w20935](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w20935/w20935.pdf)).

## Conclusion

The international evidence changes what the Colombian question should be. "Why are 22% of young Colombians NINI?" bundles at least three processes with different clocks. The first is a short graduation-age transition, shaped by institutions that switch at 18. The second is a long-lived female care state, rooted in early unions and lost schooling more than in motherhood's direct effect on work. The third is a cyclical male churn through informal jobs. The central bank's synthetic-cohort persistence cannot separate them, and policies aimed at one, such as liquidity support for tertiary entry, will barely move the others.

The highest-return next step is not a causal design but an individual-level flow decomposition by sex, type and age at graduation, using ELCA, PILA and the Saber 11 linkages. Two causal designs then stand out as contributions with no international precedent: a school-entry-cutoff RD on post-graduation NINI spells, which tests the waiting room, and a composition test of the flat cohort effect. The older-daughter channel is the most original gender hypothesis available, but its sign is genuinely uncertain. It should be framed as a test between time substitution and maternal bargaining, not as a presumed effect.
